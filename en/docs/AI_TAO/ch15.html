Chapter 15: The Weight of Passion — The Bill for Pursuing What You Love

One Sentence, Three Lives​

"Pursuing what you love matters more than following the crowd."

This sentence circulates periodically. Most recently, the person who said it was the daughter of the CEO of one of the world's highest-valued technology companies. Her story was packaged as an inspirational template: originally headed for electrical engineering, she switched to culinary arts after a single remark from her father, became a chef, a sommelier, worked in luxury brand marketing, and eventually entered the AI industry to become a core member of the company.

The story is moving. But the background conditions of this story can be replicated by perhaps only tens of thousands of people on the planet.

Because her father helms a company at the zenith of its power. She could spend more than a decade exploring culinary arts, wine, and the luxury goods industry, then return to the family enterprise as an executive at any time. Her "pursuit of passion" came with a return ticket — she could always go home, and home would only keep growing.

This is not a criticism of her. She may have genuinely loved culinary arts, and may have later genuinely developed an interest in AI. But when a person with an unlimited safety net tells you to "pursue what you love," and a person who must calculate their expenses every month makes the same choice, the risk structures of the two are entirely different.

One sentence, three lives.

The first kind: a person with a safety net talks about "pursuing passion." They are sharing a life philosophy. Risk approaches zero. If it fails, just go home.

The second kind: a person with some means but no safety net talks about "pursuing passion." It is a calculated gamble. Every day is a tightrope walk between passion and bills.

The third kind: a person without even breathing room hears "pursue what you love." It is a cruel bowl of chicken soup. They lack even the margin to choose.

Bills Do Not Disappear Because You Have Passion​

Chapter 14's "making a choice" requires almost no courage for the first kind of person. For the second kind, the choice itself is a bill that never stops coming due.

The typical trajectory of the second kind looks like this: years spent in a particular industry, touching nearly every position from R&D to production to sales to management. Not because they enjoy jumping around, but because they keep searching for a position where they can preserve their conscience. At each new role they think: maybe if I am a bit further from the core, I will not have to participate in those things. Maybe switching departments, switching companies, switching industries will change the situation.

Ultimately they discover that the "safe" position many people imagine no longer exists. The industry is shrinking; the culture is deteriorating. As the pie gets smaller and those fighting over it grow fiercer, the cost of "maintaining a conscience" climbs higher and higher. And the most unbearable thing is not what others do — it is a more fundamental question: when you have seen something that should not have happened, there is no "safe position of looking the other way" — because looking the other way is itself complicity — and you face a binary choice: stay and become the thing you despise, or leave.

Leaving is not a decisive act. Your bottom line is tested once; you hold firm. A second time, a third, a fourth — each one wears you down. Not wearing down ability, but wearing down your trust in yourself. You begin to wonder: am I still holding the line, or has the line been retreating without my noticing? The last straw is not necessarily the worst incident, but it is the one that breaks you — not because the matter is so heinous, but because you finally admit: if I stay any longer, the next person to be changed will be me.

At that point, leaving is no longer a choice; it is survival.

Then you begin writing, analysing, building your own content platform. But pursuing passion does not equal financial freedom. Every meal costs money, every computer used for work costs money, every month's rent, utilities, and insurance cost money. Income is unstable. The outside world sees "a person pursuing their passion"; what it does not see are the bills behind the scenes.

And this is already relatively fortunate — at least there is some savings buffer, decades of technical experience as a foundation, and a partner's understanding and support. Many people do not have even these.

You can simultaneously possess a fierce sense of mission and persistent financial anxiety. The two are not contradictory, but their coexistence is heavier than any inspirational story tells you. Whenever the bills come at the end of the month, "pursuing what you love" temporarily exits the stage, replaced by a very concrete question: what about next month? Passion will not pay your rent.

The Third Kind: Even Choice Is a Luxury​

The young person on the convenience-store night shift wants to learn coding during the day, but after the shift, they just want to sleep — their body cannot hold up. A single mother with a talent for painting has all her time consumed by picking up kids and working two jobs. A factory-line worker with an instinct for music has never touched a decent instrument, because that is a month's food budget. A fresh graduate burdened with student loans, parents waiting for remittances — the only choice available is which job pays more.

To tell these people "pursuing what you love matters more than following the crowd" is not inspirational; it is an insult. Not because they lack passion, but because passion requires a minimum survival threshold to be practised. You must first survive before you are entitled to talk about living as yourself.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs is cruelly accurate here: when a person is still struggling for physiological and safety needs, talking to them about self-actualisation is like lecturing a drowning person on the aesthetics of swimming.

So what should people without a safety net do? The honest answer is not pleasant. Not everyone has the conditions to pursue their passion right now. Acknowledging this reality is not giving up; it is lucidity. Surviving first is not shameful. In the process of surviving, preserving the awareness that "I know what I truly want to do" is itself a form of resistance — many people have even that awareness ground away by the pressure of survival. And pursuing passion need not be full-time. A person with only one free hour a day, if they use that hour to do what they truly care about — write a passage, sketch a drawing, learn a chord — is already pursuing their passion. The difference is not how much time you spend, but whether, in that time, you make a genuine choice.

But these answers still remain at the individual level. The real question is: why is the third kind's predicament not an isolated case but an increasingly massive demographic? This is not about individuals not trying hard enough — this is a systemic problem.

The Class Blind Spot of "Pursuing Passion"​

Here lies a structural issue that is almost never discussed: the very concept of "pursuing what you love" carries implicit class assumptions.

It assumes you have the freedom to choose — you can pick A or pick B. It assumes you have room for trial and error — choosing wrong allows a do-over. It assumes you have a surplus of time — you can spend years exploring what you actually like. It assumes you have a basic economic cushion — at the very least, you will not starve before finding your way.

These assumptions, for the majority of the world's population, do not hold — not a single one.

And when those who possess all four assumptions stand before the camera and tell you to "pursue what you love," and their stories are packaged as inspirational content and pushed to every phone by the algorithm — what is the effect? The effect is to make those who lack these conditions feel their lives are failures.

"The reason you are unhappy is that you lack the courage to pursue your passion." — The implicit logic of this sentence is: if you are not doing well, it is your own choice, not a problem of the system.

This is the most refined form of class blindness. It is not discrimination, not mockery, but an unconscious, well-intentioned, yet structurally inequality-ignoring narrative. The information feudalism dismantled in Chapter 5 appears here in its gentlest guise — it does not oppress you with fear; it oppresses you with inspiration.

Those who cannot see the lock will not think to open it. And those standing on the rooftop saying "pursue what you love" — children of entrepreneurs, heirs of the propertied class, the fortunate born with a safety net — most of them genuinely believe what they are saying. But their sincerity is precisely part of the problem: because they genuinely believe "pursue what you love" is universal advice, they cannot see the lock.

Three Weights of Passion​

The same choice to "pursue what you love" weighs entirely differently on different people.

For those with a safety net, the weight of passion approaches zero. It is a piece of luggage that can be set down at any time — when tired, just go home; someone is there to receive you.

For those with some means, the weight of passion is continuous pressure. Every day is a calculation: how long can I hold out this month? Where is the next income coming from? Your passion is not luggage; it is a stone you carry across the river — set it down and you sink; hold it and it is heavy.

For those without even breathing room, the weight of passion is infinite. Because it cannot even be picked up. It is neither luggage nor stone; it is the scenery on the other bank of the river — you can see it, but between you lies a river you can never swim across.

When discussing "pursuing passion," if you do not simultaneously discuss "who bears the cost of pursuing passion," you are merely producing inspirational content for the privileged.

Why "Passion" Is Still Worth Discussing​

Having written so much about the weight of passion, why is it still considered worth discussing?

Not because it guarantees success, nor because it is a better path. But because in the AI era, it may be humanity's last remaining irreplaceability.

Chapter 10 dismantled the five layers of scarcity — tools are no longer scarce; direction is. Answers are no longer scarce; judgement is. Content is no longer scarce; trust is. In a world where all standardisable skills will be done better, faster, and cheaper by AI, the only thing that cannot be replaced is the output a person produces when doing what they truly care about — output that fuses personal experience, emotional memory, aesthetic judgement, and value choices. Chapter 12 located this with the word-image-meaning framework: this is "meaning." It cannot be generated; it can only be lived.

But there is a precondition: you must genuinely care. Not "the market needs this so I do it," but "even if the market does not need it, I would still do it." This degree of caring only appears in the state of pursuing passion.

So the contradiction is right here: the thing the AI era needs most — authentic human passion — is precisely what this era's economic structure least supports. The market rewards efficiency, scale, and replicability. Passion is inefficient, personal, and unreplicable.

This is the ultimate meaning of "the weight of passion": it is heavy not because passion itself is heavy, but because the entire system is pressing down on it.

But why does the system press down on it? Why does the same effort not yield the returns it once did? Why is the third kind's predicament not the result of personal choices but a structural product?

This is not a question about "whether the effort is enough." It is a question about "whether the system you are in still allows effort to be exchanged for reward."

The weight of the bill varies from person to person. But there is a question more fundamental than any personal bill: why does the same effort no longer yield the returns it once did?
