📌 Those who have never experienced immense pressure rarely realize that human judgment can be systematically eroded by environments. Gaslighting, peer pressure, and artificial urgency are not exclusive to scams, but cognitive traps present in daily life.

How Environments Make People Foolish—Cognition and Judgment (Postscript)

Victims Aren't Necessarily Foolish​

Every time a pyramid scheme or mass investment scam is exposed, the public's first reaction is almost universally the same: "How could they be so foolish to believe that?"

In 2025, Hong Kong police received over 5,000 reports of online investment scams—a sharp 30% increase from the previous year—involving losses amounting to HK$3.58 billion, with an average loss of HK$700,000 per case. The JPEX case accumulated reports from over 2,700 victims involving approximately HK$1.6 billion. The victims included many highly educated individuals, those with stable careers, and even professionals working in the financial sector itself.

If "foolishness" could explain everything, these figures wouldn't exist.

The real problem was never IQ. It was the environment.

A Design Flaw in the Brain​

Human decision-making mechanisms have a "slow lane" and a "fast lane."

The fast lane is dominated by the amygdala—the brain's threat detection center. It can trigger the fight-or-flight response before our conscious mind even registers it, releasing adrenaline and cortisol, pushing the body into a state of emergency. The slow lane routes through the prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for rational analysis, evaluating consequences, and weighing pros and cons.

Under normal circumstances, the prefrontal cortex overrides the amygdala's impulsive responses, allowing humans to make deliberate decisions. But the problem lies here: when stress, fear, or fatigue exceeds a certain threshold, the amygdala hijacks the entire decision-making system. Psychologist Daniel Goleman coined this phenomenon the "amygdala hijack" in 1995.

Neuroscientific research further indicates that continuous high cognitive load can diminish emotional regulation capability by up to 40%. In other words, an individual with typically strong judgment can see a significant decline in decision-making quality under prolonged high-pressure environments—not because they've become foolish, but because their brain has been forced to switch to a mode unfit for complex judgments.

This isn't a character flaw. This is a physiological mechanism.

Manipulators Target Speed, Not Intelligence​

The design logic of scams is never about being "smarter than you," but being "faster than you."

All highly efficient scam structures share the same operational principle: creating a sense of urgency to strip away cool-down time. "If you don't enter today, you lose your spot," "Only three slots left," "The price doubles after tonight"—the function of these rhetorical tactics isn't to persuade you, but to activate your amygdala, prompting you to make a decision before your prefrontal cortex has time to intervene.

However, low-level scams only use a sense of urgency; high-level scams use environments.

The reason pyramid schemes and mass investment scams are far more harmful is precisely because they aren't one-on-one deceptions, but the reshaping of a person by an entire environment. When a victim is immersed within it, every signal they receive reinforces the exact same narrative: everyone around them is investing, everyone is making money, everyone is excited, and everyone believes this is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity.

When you look around and everyone else "believes," you start to doubt your own doubts.

The Collective Version of Gaslighting​

The term "gaslighting" originally describes one-on-one psychological manipulation—where the manipulator persistently denies the victim's memory, perception, and judgment, causing the victim to gradually lose faith in their own cognitive abilities.

But when this mechanism is transplanted into a group environment, its destructive power grows exponentially.

Within the closed communities of pyramid organizations or investment scams, skepticism itself gets redefined. You aren't being "cautious"; you "lack vision." You aren't "analyzing rationally"; you "don't trust the team." You aren't protecting yourself; you are "missing out on a chance to change your destiny."

The concept of "groupthink," introduced by social psychologist Irving Janis in 1972, precisely describes this process: once high cohesion is formed within a group, dissent is systematically suppressed—not through violence, but through social pressure. Members self-censor, swallowing their doubts because the social cost of expressing dissent (being isolated, labeled negatively, ostracized) is far higher than the cost of remaining silent.

At a certain tipping point, the "consensus" within the group is entirely divorced from reality, yet no one dares to point it out because everyone assumes they are the only one having doubts. This isn't collective stupidity; this is structural cognitive suppression.

"Artificial Urgency": The Tactic Shared by Corporations and Scams​

It's worth noting that this manipulation tactic is not exclusive to scams.

In formal business environments, "artificial urgency" is likewise a common management and negotiation tool. Demanding employees to make major commitments in extremely short timeframes, signing terms while exhausted, yielding objections under the pressure that "everyone else agrees"—these scenarios occur daily in legitimate companies.

The difference lies in degree, not in nature.

Scams push these tactics to the extreme: completely enclosed information environments, sustained emotional high pressure, systematic isolation of skeptics, and drowning out individual rationality with the fanaticism of the crowd. Yet their underlying logic shares similarities with certain high-pressure decision scenarios in the workplace—both rely on stripping away calm reflection time to degrade the quality of judgment.

Recognizing this is crucial because it means you don't need to be in a pyramid scheme to fall for it. Any environment that does not allow you to be "half a beat slower" is exerting pressure on your judgment.

It doesn't even require deliberate manipulation—the physical space of an office conference room itself degrades judgment. The presence of a boss, the gaze of colleagues, a rushed agenda, the expectation of immediate responses—these factors stack up to create an implicit cognitive pressure, forcing you to declare a stance before you've thought things through. For many decisions agreed upon in conference rooms, if you went home, sat down, and thought calmly, you'd realize you would never have agreed to them. This isn't because you became stupider in the meeting room, but because that environment systematically compressed your thinking space. Conversely, the reason remote work (WFH) allows some people to make better judgments isn't that being home is more comfortable, but because physical distance naturally insulates them from group pressure, giving the prefrontal cortex a chance to function normally. You don't need to respond immediately under everyone's gaze; you can turn off your camera, think for three minutes, and then speak. Those three minutes are your space for clarity.

The Operational Definition of Clarity​

If environments can make people foolish, then maintaining clarity can't rely solely on "willpower" or "IQ"—it requires concrete operational methods.

The first rule is to distinguish types of judgments. Daily decisions—what to eat for lunch, which tool to use, which email to reply to—can be made instantly because the cost of being wrong is low and opportunities for correction are plentiful. But major decisions—involving large sums of money, long-term commitments, or irreversible consequences—must have cool-down space. The ability to accurately distinguish between these two types of judgments is, in itself, a high-level cognitive skill.

The second rule is to identify the source of pressure. When someone urges you to make a decision, first ask one question: Does the urgency of this matter exist objectively, or was it manufactured by the other party? If it's the latter, what you need is not to make a decision faster, but to refuse making a decision under pressure more firmly.

The third rule is the simplest, yet the hardest to execute: sleep on it before deciding. This is not a metaphor. Neuroscience has confirmed that sleep resets the function of the prefrontal cortex, restoring cognitive resources depleted by stress. A fully rested brain has a fundamental difference in judgment quality compared to a fatigued brain. If possible, all major decisions should be made after sleeping on them.

Missing Out vs. Messing Up​

The cost of being "half a beat slower" is real—you will miss out on opportunities. In hindsight, some of those opportunities were indeed real and could have actually made you money.

But the worst-case scenario of missing an opportunity is simply earning less. The worst-case scenario of messing up a major decision is losing years of accumulated capital, or even entirely disrupting the rhythm of your life.

The risk structures of the two are completely asymmetrical.

In the framework of infinite games—where life is not a single match determining a winner, but a long-term game where you need to continuously stay on the field—avoiding maximum risk is far more important than pursuing maximum profit. You must first ensure you don't get knocked out before you can talk about how to win.

Many victims of mass investment scams gave up the protective mechanism of being "half a beat slower" precisely because they were pursuing a "get rich quick" mentality. They weren't foolish. They were simply stripped of the conditions to make clear judgments within a meticulously designed environment.

Conclusion​

The next time you see news about a pyramid scheme or mass investment scam, before saying "How could they be so foolish to believe that?", you might want to think for a moment: if it were you in that exact same environment—with everyone around you investing, all signals urging you on, all skepticism suppressed—how confident are you that you wouldn't waver?

The answer might not be as certain as you think.

And acknowledging this is, in itself, the beginning of clarity.

If you understand what this article is conveying, then from now on, please constantly remind yourself of one thing: in the face of any major decision, preserve your own time for clarity. This is your final, and most important, line of defense.
