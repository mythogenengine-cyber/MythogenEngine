Author: Nebula Walker Date: 17MAY2026 MYTHOGEN ENGINE (mythogenengine.com)

📌 Deconstructing the business logic behind AI courses that sell 'simplicity' and how they shift the responsibility of failure entirely onto the learner.

When "Education" Becomes a Business That Cannot Fail

I recently wrote an article about the "one-person company," and the core point was: it's not a burst of opportunities, but a structural contraction.

After writing it, a deeper question kept turning in my mind:

Why is it that in every technological wave, the first people to make money are always those selling courses?

Over a decade ago, it was robotics, LEGO education, and children's programming. Later, it was blockchain, NFTs, and Web3. Now, it's AI.

The tools have changed, but the routine is exactly the same.

And I myself used to teach over a decade ago.

So I want to break this down from the perspective of someone who has taught.

1. I've Taught, So I Know What a "Clear-Cut Transaction" Is​

Many years ago, I did tutoring and skill teaching.

Does teaching guarantee the student will learn it? Not necessarily. Tutoring has never been about guaranteed results.

But one thing is clear: Everyone knows what the transaction is.

Just like learning to drive—nobody guarantees you'll get a license, but you know what you're learning, what you're being tested on, and what the standards are. The person teaching knows, and the person learning knows. Whether you can actually hit the road after learning is one thing, but the fact that "you are learning to drive" leaves absolutely no room for ambiguity.

It was the same when I taught. I'd teach a skill, the student would practice, and whether they could do it after practicing, both sides knew the score. When they couldn't do it, I wouldn't tell them to "enjoy the process of correction"—I'd wonder if my teaching method needed adjustment, if the pace needed to slow down, or if I had overestimated their foundation.

This kind of teaching isn't sexy, it's not easily scalable, and the profit margin isn't high.

But it has one trait: It's a clear-cut transaction (明買明賣).

You know what you're buying, and the teacher knows what they're selling. Both sides have a consistent understanding of this matter.

2. What Are They Selling Now?​

Open Facebook, Threads, or X, and you'll see overwhelming course advertisements:

"AI Tools Practical Class" "Even those with zero foundation can learn" "Wife and kids can easily get the hang of it"

The prices aren't cheap, often running into the tens of thousands.

But look closely, and you'll notice something—

These courses almost never talk about "what you can concretely achieve after learning."

What they talk about is:

  • It's very simple
  • No foundation required
  • Just follow along
  • I did it, and so can you

Sounds reasonable. But there is a structural problem hidden in this.

3. "Simple" Is More Poisonous Than "Making $100k a Month"​

Many people think the easiest trap to fall into is the promise-based courses claiming "make 100k a month, switch careers successfully, become a one-person company."

Actually, no.

For that kind of big talk, most people will still harbor some doubt. You'll do the math, think "is this real?", at least your rationality is still functioning.

What really consumes the most people is the layer selling "simplicity."

"No foundation required" "Even kids can learn it" "Just follow the steps"

These phrases bypass your rational judgment and directly hit a more fundamental psychology—

👉 "I'm afraid I won't be able to learn it."

When a course tells you "you will definitely be able to learn it," it's not selling a skill; it's selling a promise: you won't be stumped.

And this promise is almost impossible to hold accountable.

Because if you can't do it after learning, the conclusion is always "you didn't work hard enough," not that there's a problem with the course.

4. "Huo Qubing": A Perfect Demonstration​

Recently, there was a case that perfectly demonstrated this entire chain.

The AI short drama "Huo Qubing" went viral online. The promotional narrative went like this: 3,000 RMB cost, a 3-person team, completed in 48 hours, and 500 million views. Massive armies, flying dust, cinematic visuals—all generated by AI.

Sound familiar? "Simple," "low cost," "anyone can do it."

But later, the production team itself came out to clarify: the so-called 3,000 RMB was just the computing cost, not including manpower; the team wasn't 3 people, it was nearly 20; and the "500 million views" figure was actually only in the hundreds of thousands on overseas platforms, and the claimed 80 episodes simply don't exist on any video platform.

In other words—every single attractive number in the promotion was inflated.

But that's not the point. The point is what happened next.

Based on this inflated "successful case," courses immediately followed. Open Bilibili, and you can already see:

"Used AI to make the viral short drama 'Huo Qubing', over 500 million views, cost only 3 thousand, the entire process shared selflessly! Step-by-step teaching, all hard knowledge, no nonsense! Learn it easily!"

See it?

A "result" with inherently flawed data, stripped of all real conditions, turned into "you can do it too." And then the course sellers entered the market.

This is the complete chain: Exaggerate results → Omit conditions → Package as "simple" → Launch a course and collect money.

And if you follow it and fail... then what?

5. After the Failure Comes the Most Brilliant Part​

If it just ended in failure, this business model wouldn't be considered perfect.

What's truly brilliant is: the failure itself has been co-opted.

When you learn it and can't do it, you won't arrive at the conclusion that "the course has a problem." You will get:

"This is just part of the process." "Occasional failures are normal." "Enjoy the process of correction." "Learning isn't achieved overnight."

Sounds very positive, inspiring, and reasonable.

But think clearly: What is the function of this rhetoric?

Its function is—to make this course forever unfalsifiable.

  • Succeeded? The course is useful.
  • Failed? You're still on the journey.
  • Keep failing? You need more practice (and by the way, we recommend the advanced class).

This is a logical closed loop. Once you enter this closed loop, there is no result that will allow you to say those four words: "I was scammed."

Because the judgment of "being scammed" has been preemptively sterilized.

6. The Gray Area of "Willing Buyer, Willing Seller"​

Some might say: What's the point of talking about this? It's not illegal, it's a willing buyer and a willing seller.

I've heard this many times. Relatives have asked, friends have asked, and I've even asked myself.

"You clearly know how this business is played, why don't you get involved yourself?"

Logically, there's indeed no problem: it's legal, there's a market, there's demand, and you have the ability.

But "legal" and a "clear-cut transaction" are two different things.

The law governs the bottom line—you can't commit fraud, you can't falsely advertise (though many toe the line).

But the layer the law can't reach is where the problem lies:

👉 Is what the buyer thinks they are buying the same thing as what they actually receive?

If a person spends 15,000 on an "AI Practical Class," thinking "after learning this I'll be able to use AI to make things," but actually receives "ten recorded videos, three templates, and a chat group"—

This isn't fraud, but it's not a clear-cut transaction either.

This is a kind of "expectation management technique": managing your expectations during sales so you feel you're getting a great deal; lowering the standards during delivery so you feel what you got isn't too bad either.

The gap in between is the profit.

7. The True Dividing Line​

So the issue isn't "whether courses are useful," "whether AI is worth learning," or "whether education should charge fees."

None of these are the point.

The point is only one line:

Is what you promote and the effect you claim consistent with what the student actually gets?

This is a clear-cut transaction.

When I taught over a decade ago, I never needed to tell a student to "enjoy the process of correction." Because whether they could learn it was one thing, but "what we are teaching and what we are learning" was never ambiguous between us. When a student couldn't do it, my first reaction was to review my teaching method, not to tell them to "work harder."

Good education doesn't guarantee results, but it has a very simple characteristic: Both sides have a consistent understanding of this transaction, and the person teaching is willing to take responsibility for the teaching quality.

Can't learn it? First, think about whether it's a problem with the teaching method. Can't do it? First, think about whether the difficulty setting is wrong. Student gave up? First, think about whether the pacing is off.

The person teaching will at least ask themselves first, rather than immediately pushing the responsibility back.

But many courses today have completely reversed this responsibility:

Can't learn it? You're not working hard enough. Can't do it? You need more time. Gave up? You didn't persevere.

The responsibility is always on the student.

This is the most fundamental difference. Not the price, not the format, not whether it's online or offline—it's about who bears the responsibility.

8. So What Do We Do? Browse in Bookstores​

Having talked about so many problems, I need to offer a solution.

This method isn't new, it isn't sexy, and it isn't for everyone, but it's what I've always used myself:

Go to a bookstore, browse, and buy books.

When a brand-new field emerges—whether it's AI, blockchain, or anything you completely don't understand—you don't need to immediately spend tens of thousands on a course. You can wait.

Wait for what? Wait for the knowledge and technology to mature slightly, wait for introductory and advanced books to appear on the market.

Then you go to a bookstore, flip through the table of contents, read a few sample pages, and see which book suits your current level. Buy one or two first, spend a few hundred HKD or one or two thousand NTD, to lay down a foundation and figure out the basic concepts and frameworks of this field.

Once you have this base, then you go look at free online resources, AI tool tutorials, and in-depth magazine reports—by this time, you already have the ability to judge what content is valuable and what is a waste of your time.

Finally, if you really need to take a course, you also have the ability to judge whether a course is worth that price.

Because you already have a foundation, you know whether what the course is teaching is something you've already read in a book, whether it truly has advanced content, or if it's just packaging free information to sell to you.

Books, of course, aren't omnipotent. But they have one advantage: Before you pay, just by flipping through a few pages, you already know what you are buying.

9. But Judgment Is the True Threshold​

Is this step difficult? Yes. Especially for people who don't have a habit of reading; groping around the knowledge structure of an unfamiliar field on your own is indeed not easy.

But ask the reverse: if you can't even cross this step, will paying money really allow you to buy knowledge?

Let me add a fair remark in passing: not all paid learning is selling anxiety. Industry seminars are an example—they are usually expensive, companies mostly foot the bill, and the content is sharing and trend analysis by industry insiders. It doesn't teach you "zero-foundation basics" because the target audience isn't zero-foundation people in the first place. It's not tricking you; it just has specific goals and audiences. The problem is never whether the thing itself is good or bad, it's whether you have the ability to judge if it's suitable for you.

Final Thoughts​

This article isn't saying that all courses are scams, nor is it saying AI isn't worth learning.

Even books are the same—bookstores are also filled with "Learn XX in 30 Days" and "The XX You Must Know in the AI Era," with covers selling fear. The benefit of a book isn't that its content is definitely good; it's that you can flip through it before deciding to buy, so the cost of judgment is low. But books themselves aren't a panacea.

Courses, books, seminars, free resources—the medium isn't the point.

The point is whether you have judgment and know what you need.

And judgment isn't innate; it's trained. It relies on you reading, trying, and groping around to slowly figure out the basic structure of a field. There are no shortcuts in this process, and no one can do it for you.

So what this article ultimately wants to say is actually just one sentence:

Before you spend your money, first learn how to learn on your own.
