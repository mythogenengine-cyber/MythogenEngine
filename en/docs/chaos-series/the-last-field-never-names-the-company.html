Where Does the Chaos Go? (4): The Last Field Never Names the Company

@Mythogen Engine · Nov 23, 2026

The previous article, "Each Time the Problem Gets Worse, Someone Gets Promoted," argued that the monopoly margin pays for the chaos inside organizations, and that when it can no longer cover it, the cost falls on people. This is the last article in the series, about whom that bill finally lands on.

Prologue: The Fields on a Record​

When a person breaks down at work, or dies, there is always a record. The record needs a cause, and there are ready-made fields for it: debt, relationships, mental state, resilience.

There is no field called "how the company treated them."

Without that field, nothing gets counted; and when nothing is counted, every case becomes an isolated incident. To society it is a news item that fades in a few days. To the person it happens to, it is crying out with no one to hear.

This is the same mechanism as the Jira intake in the first article, only magnified to the scale of society: data records only what has a category, and whatever has no category might as well never have happened. This series has followed the chaos as it moves: created by promises, landing first on the desktop, absorbed into the organization, paid for by the monopoly margin. The last stop is people.

1. The Structure Needs No Villains, but It Selects for Them​

This series, together with Who's Collecting at the Door, has held one position throughout: the structure can run without villains. But that sentence is easily misread as "there are no bad people."

A more accurate statement is: the structure does not need bad people in order to run, but it selects for them, rewards them, and even pushes some who were not bad into becoming so. Fire one and the next one in the seat produces the same result, because the position itself was designed for that kind of person and the selection mechanism has not changed. Factions are the same: only the faces change, the rules stay.

Those people have their own circumstances too. They are married, with children, have borrowed at high interest to buy an expensive flat in Taipei, cannot afford to lose the job, and their family may fall apart at any moment. They lack what chapter three of Who's Collecting at the Door calls a "floor": without a floor, they cannot afford to lose, and when they cannot afford to lose, there is nothing they will not do. Chapter one describes how companies take away an engineer's ability to say "this won't work"; high leverage and the lack of a floor take away a person's ability to say "no" morally.

They are hostages themselves, and in the end hostages go and take someone else hostage. Taking credit, coordinating stories, slowly grinding people out: all of it is about keeping their own place. I never imagined a single company could hold so many people who feel they have no choice and will therefore do anything. Only later did I understand: however many people in a company have no floor, that is how many are ready to do anything at all for the boss or the system.

2. Understanding Is Not Forgiving​

Understanding their circumstances does not mean forgiving them. Their reasons and what they did to others are two different things.

Having your credit taken, stories coordinated against you, being slowly ground out: none of this can happen without leaving marks. Many people, years after leaving, still carry the shadow and are still angry when those few names come up, and that is entirely reasonable. Structure can explain how harm happens, but it does not make the harm any less real. If "the structure needs no villains" is used to tell victims not to be angry, the sentence is being misused.

Then why not name names, or expose them? In theory one could. Expose them and they all lose their jobs; but the position will be filled by the next person, the selection mechanism will not have changed at all, and the person who exposed them carries all the cost without gaining anything.

Writing about structure rather than people is not evasion. People change, companies get renamed, bad actors move on to the next firm; but a description of the structure will still hold ten years from now. Name one person and in ten years it is old news; explain clearly how a structure selects and produces such people, and the next reader who runs into them will recognize them.

3. Consequences Recorded as Causes​

Back to that record. The cruellest part is the direction of cause and effect.

The circumstances in section one: a high-interest mortgage, a job you cannot lose, a family that might fall apart. Debt and broken relationships are very often themselves results of work pressure: no time to go home, constant overtime, afraid to stop for fear of losing the job. But on the record they become the causes. Once consequences are recorded as causes, the real cause disappears. Chapter six of Who's Collecting at the Door noted that correlation is not causation and that causation can be reversed; this is its heaviest form.

Outside, the accounts usually come in only two kinds, pointing in opposite directions but with the same effect. One calls the person a hero who sacrificed themselves for the job; the other says they lacked resilience, that they were not strong enough. Neither account needs to ask a single question: what happened inside the company?

As for those who did not die, who leave with damaged bodies and minds, the record shows a "voluntary resignation," which leaves even less trace. Chapter five of Who's Collecting at the Door discussed "voluntary resignation"; here we see its other side: those who leave on their own also carry the cost on their own.

4. The Evidence Is in the Company's Hands​

To prove that someone's breakdown was connected to the company, almost all the evidence needed is in the company's hands: emails, performance records, meetings, who said what, who was assigned what. Families and outsiders cannot get it; the colleagues who could get it each have their own reasons not to speak, because they too have mortgages, children, and no floor.

Chapter six of Who's Collecting at the Door discussed the cost of verification: successful prevention cannot be verified, while failure can be counted. Here, the cost of verification is counted in lives.

Whether a category exists makes a big difference. Japan wrote "death from overwork" into law: the Act on Promotion of Measures to Prevent Death and Injury from Overwork was passed in 2014 and took effect that November, and the government has since had to report on overwork deaths to the Diet every year.¹ With that category, at least some cases can be seen and counted. But even with the category, for a case to be recognized as an occupational injury the family must still prove the working hours and workload; the burden of proof is heavy, and only some cases are recognized.

So the question "how many cases have been covered up?" cannot be answered, structurally. The numbers that could answer it are themselves numbers that have been filtered.

5. Confidentiality Seals the Last Exit​

Add confidentiality, and the company is essentially invincible.

That the evidence is in the company's hands is a fact; confidentiality agreements turn that fact into a legal obligation. Current employees sign confidentiality clauses; departing employees trade their severance for a non-disparagement agreement; settlements with families can carry confidentiality terms too. Internal investigations are kept private "to protect the privacy of those involved," so even the victim's privacy can become the company's shield. The information society would use to count these cases is sealed away, one agreement at a time.

As with every mechanism in this series, each step has a reason. Confidentiality exists to protect trade secrets and personal privacy, and there is nothing wrong with that. The problem is that its scope slowly widens, from protecting technology to wrapping up how the company treats its employees as well. Nobody has to design this use; as long as every agreement is drafted by lawyers as broadly as possible, the effect emerges on its own.

But it is not completely invincible. Some places have begun to limit the scope of confidentiality agreements. The Speak Out Act, signed in the United States in 2022, makes pre-signed confidentiality and non-disparagement clauses unenforceable in disputes over sexual harassment and sexual assault.² But it covers only sexual harassment and assault, and only clauses signed before the dispute, not settlement agreements afterwards. Other routes include labour inspections, disclosure of evidence in litigation, and whistleblower protection. All of them are slow and expensive, and they require the person involved to accept the risk, so in practice most cases never get that far.

6. Where Does What Automation Saves Go?​

At this point I have to describe a change in myself. I do not know whether it counts as growth or as something bad.

The old me pushed automation without a second thought. If it should be done, do it, because I firmly believed in a theory: keeping people on through inefficiency is like ploughing a field with a spoon. People who are displaced will naturally find new work. All I needed was to be one of the best, and help companies get rid of those old systems.

But AI has made one question impossible to avoid. Suppose legacy really can be removed with AI, the product gets better and the company more efficient; then firing the people who maintained the legacy seems perfectly justified. The work of many of them really did consist of lining people up to stop other people from getting things done. But very few new people are coming in either. Where does the extra money the company makes go?

I thought about it for a long time, and eventually realized my theory was not wrong; it just had a hidden premise. "Displaced people will naturally find new work" requires the money from efficiency to flow back into new demand and new positions, and faster than people can retrain. I spent most of my career in expanding companies, where the resources saved by automation really did become new product lines and new seats. Later I saw shrinking, financialized companies, where the savings became margins, buybacks and reported figures, not new seats. It is not that I changed; it is that the conditions under which the theory holds changed.

Is distribution an engineer's business? Historically, efficiency gains turned into shorter working hours not because engineers won them inside companies, but through forces outside companies: labour law, unions and taxation. In 1930 Keynes predicted that a century on, productivity gains would mean people needed to work only fifteen hours a week.³ The productivity came; the working hours did not fall that far. A single engineer inside a company cannot solve distribution; but choosing which kind of company to help, which kind of project to work on, is within an engineer's own say.

In theory, when more money is made, everyone could work a little less for the same pay. What forbids "paying people more to do less" is financialization, not society's resources. If resources were short, that would be an engineering problem, solvable by technology; if the structure forbids it, that is a question of values. Engineering can answer "how"; it cannot answer "for whom." So in the end everything turned toward philosophy, and that is not evasion. The question itself leads there.

7. Why Write It as a Novel​

I wrote a novel called Mirror Realm. Some people may ask why I did not write it as non-fiction. The previous sections are the answer.

The record has no such field, the evidence is in the company's hands, confidentiality agreements seal the specific facts, and the more specific non-fiction is, the easier it is to identify the people involved, and the easier it is to dismiss with "you don't understand the internal situation." But confidentiality can seal facts, not structures: no company names, no real people, no details that point to anyone, only a description of how something works. With most exits sealed, fiction is one of the few paths still open.

More importantly, what statistics cannot count, a novel can make people feel. The media-light effect in Mirror Realm is not about someone lying; it is that even what the people involved "know" to be right and wrong is not true. Narrative monopoly is at its most complete when even the victims believe the problem is theirs. Analysis can explain that mechanism clearly, but it cannot convey its weight.

So this series and Mirror Realm are two sides of the same thing. One helps readers recognize the structure; the other lets them feel its weight.

Coda: The Last Field​

Across four articles, the path of the chaos runs like this: the gap is created by promises; it lands first on the visible desktop of Excel; after the clean-up it is absorbed into the organization, settles into a monster, and the monster becomes a seat; the people guarding the seat rise as the problem worsens, and the monopoly margin pays for all of it; when it can no longer cover it, the cost is recorded against individuals.

And the share recorded against individuals is the one bill nobody passes on again. Not because it is the smallest, but because it has already reached the bottom.

If you are sitting in one of the seats this series describes, giving everything every day to workarounds, failing year after year to solve the problem, beginning to wonder whether you are simply not good enough, remember what these four articles say: that seat was designed so the job could not be done. Whether you can leave is a separate question; not treating this structure as your own defect is the point.

That record will still not carry the company's name. But at least you will know what that field should say.
