The Denser the Backstops, the Bigger the Next Crash

@Mythogen Engine · Nov 23, 2026

Where Did the Crashes Go? — Part 3 of 3

National teams, pension funds, ETFs and central banks have made crashes shorter and slower. But this system of backstops rests on premises. As those premises fail one by one, the risk that has been building up will be released in the third and fourth waves. Right now, only some of the conditions are in place.

Data as of 20 November 2026.

No dates, only the question of who gets hurt​

The previous article ended with a question: how long can the people holding the line keep holding it? This piece tries to answer. The answer is not a date. It is a set of conditions.

These three articles do not predict dates. Predicting dates is a short-term game: those who guess right are remembered, those who guess wrong are forgotten. Structure answers a different question: when the decline comes, who gets hurt, how badly, and how you can prepare. No one has ever been able to predict the day of an earthquake, yet no one calls building codes nonsense. Taiwan revised its codes after the 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake not because anyone knew when the next one would strike, but because people knew which kinds of buildings would collapse when it did. Every condition listed in this article can be checked by you, quarter by quarter. If the conditions do not appear, I will admit I was wrong.

In the stock market, "which kinds of buildings collapse" means three kinds of people. The first are retail investors who came in through ETFs and monthly savings plans. They think they bought "diversification"; what they actually bought is concentrated exposure to the same handful of heavyweight stocks. The second are margin traders, who get liquidated on the days when limit-down locks the market and they cannot sell. Their losses are not paper losses; they are principal. The third are members of Hong Kong's MPF and Taiwan's labor pension scheme who are close to retirement. Those who happened to retire in 2008 have already shown what this looks like: the index eventually came back, but not at the moment they withdrew.

A judgment without a date can still be tested. The method is to write down the conditions in advance and let time score them one by one. In my memory-chip series I wrote that the cycle was peaking, without giving a date; when the KOSPI plunged in July, readers could go back and check for themselves whether those conditions had held. This piece uses the same method. First, why the backstops keep getting denser; then, what premises they rest on; and finally a scorecard showing which conditions have already appeared, which have not, and what would prove me wrong.

How "it always comes back" became common sense​

In the 2008 financial crisis, the central banks came to the rescue. In the 2020 pandemic, central banks and governments came to the rescue together. In 2022, rate hikes brought a year of declines, and then the AI story took over. Three times the market fell, and every time those who bought the dip were rewarded. What the market learned was not "the risk is large" but "a fall is an opportunity." Today that line is written into fund-house advertising, into YouTube tutorials on monthly investing, and into the mind of everyone who is saying "it's just a correction."

The economist Hyman Minsky made a simple observation in the last century: stability itself breeds instability. The longer the calm lasts, the more people believe it will continue, so they borrow more, pledge more and hold less cash. Risk does not build up during turbulence; it builds up during calm. When the turbulence finally arrives, it is not an accident but the settling of the bill for all the calm that came before.

Yellowstone National Park is a more intuitive example. After the park was founded in 1872, it followed a policy of total fire suppression for close to a hundred years: every small fire was put out. Small fires would normally have burned away dead branches and low brush at regular intervals. Once they were suppressed, that fuel piled up year after year. In the summer of 1988, drought and strong winds arrived, and the fires burned about 790,000 acres, close to 36 percent of the park. The drought and the wind were the trigger; a century of accumulated fuel was the structure. No one can predict the day lightning strikes, but you can measure how much dry wood is lying on the ground.

Every market rescue puts out a small fire. Whatever the small fire did not burn away — excessive valuations, excessive leverage, companies that should not have survived — stays in the market, waiting for the next time.

Worse, the backstops themselves change behaviour. Economists call this moral hazard: when you know someone will catch you, you stop being careful. This year's intervention by Taiwan's National Stabilization Fund is its tenth, and to retail investors "the Stabilization Fund is meeting" is no longer an alarm but a buy signal. The US market treats the Federal Reserve the same way: "the Fed will step in" is already priced into stocks. Once a backstop is priced in, it is no longer just insurance; it becomes everyone's reason to raise their bets. The more people raise their bets, the heavier the load the backstop has to catch.

The four premises the backstops rest on​

The backstops are not unconditional. Every rescue over the past decade and more worked because the following premises held at the same time. They will not all disappear together, but if even one goes missing, the backstops lose some of their force.

First, central banks can print. The rescues of 2008 and 2020 both happened when inflation was very low, so printing money did not bite back right away. Today US inflation stood at 3.7 percent year on year in September. No annual figure was published for October, because last year's government shutdown left no base to compare against, but prices still rose 0.4 percent on the month, more than expected. The 10-year Treasury yield is above 5 percent, and at one point the market put the odds of another hike in December at around 90 percent. Rescuing the stock market from here means pouring fuel on inflation. It is not that central banks will not rescue; it is that every rescue now costs more, and they will wait longer before stepping in.

Second, there is enough ammunition. Taiwan's National Stabilization Fund can deploy up to NT$500 billion, and NT$300 billion of that is itself borrowed from postal savings, labor insurance, labor pension and civil-service pension funds. Against cumulative net foreign selling of roughly NT$3.1 trillion this year, that is only about a sixth. The Fund's job has never been to absorb every share sold; it is to steady confidence. But once confidence has to be proven with money, the size of the ammunition matters.

Third, the currency can cooperate. Hong Kong runs a currency peg, so the HKMA cannot print Hong Kong dollars to rescue the stock market. Its 1998 intervention used the Exchange Fund. Today the real buyers of Hong Kong stocks are Southbound money from the mainland, which makes up more than 40 percent of turnover, and the MPF. The first depends on mainland policy; the second is the monthly contributions of every working person in Hong Kong. Taiwan is a small open economy: when foreign money leaves, the New Taiwan dollar comes under pressure at the same time, and the central bank has to choose between defending the currency and defending the stock market. Neither market has America's luxury of printing its own money.

Fourth, what gets rescued is real value, not just nominal value. Even if the backstops succeed, the index "coming back" does not mean your money comes back. The Dow Jones first touched 1,000 in 1966 and was still hovering around that level in 1982. The index went nowhere for sixteen years, and two bouts of high inflation along the way ate away a large share of its real purchasing power. The Nikkei's high at the end of 1989 was not surpassed in nominal terms until February 2024, 34 years later. None of these were "crashes" — no single day was especially frightening — but for someone who bought at the top and retired twenty years later, the result was much the same.

So the question is not only "will there be a rescue" but "do the premises that make a rescue work still hold." Today the first premise has clearly weakened, the second and third have never been strong, and the fourth depends on whether inflation comes down.

Big declines rarely happen at once; they come in waves​

The crash most people remember is "a day": Black Monday in 1987, the circuit breakers of 2020. But what really breaks people is usually not that day; it is the year or two that follows.

  • United States, 1929–32. After the October crash, the Dow rebounded by nearly 50 percent by the following April, and many thought the worst was over. Then it fell for more than two years, losing close to 90 percent from its peak.
  • Hong Kong, 1973–74. The Hang Seng Index fell from 1,774 in March 1973 to 150 at the end of 1974, a drop of more than 90 percent. There were rebounds along the way, and each time people bought in. Those who bought the dip at 1,000 thought they had a bargain; there was still more than 80 percent left to fall.
  • United States, 2000–02. The Nasdaq fell from about 5,000 to around 1,100, a decline of nearly 80 percent, with several rebounds of more than 20 percent along the way. No single day was especially terrifying, yet cumulatively it was a complete destruction.

These three share one feature: the first wave was not the deepest. Every rebound drew a new group of people back in, who were then hurt in the next wave. By today's definitions, most of the legs in these declines would have been called mere "corrections."

In the process, the backstops change. In the first wave of rescue, the ammunition is full, confidence is fresh, and the effect is strongest. By the second wave, three things are different: part of the ammunition has been used; the rescuers have themselves become shareholders, and the market knows they will have to sell eventually; and "the government will rescue" has been said once already, so it carries less weight the second time. The third and fourth waves are harder to rescue than the first, not because they fall faster but because the rescuers are already tired.

For retail investors, there is a deeply unfair asymmetry here. You can buy the dip right nine times and wrong once. The nine wins each make a little; the one loss, if it happens to be the first wave of a multi-wave decline and you are using leverage, can wipe out all the past profits at once. A phenomenon behavioural finance calls the "disposition effect" makes this worse: people tend to sell winning stocks too early and hold on to losing ones, even adding to them. Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean studied the full trading records of the Taiwan market and found that individual investors as a group lost about 3.8 percentage points a year, a total equal to 2.2 percent of Taiwan's GDP. Those are figures from ordinary years, not crash years.

This year's Taiwan market is a live example. The first article set out three corrections in six months; each was interrupted by rebounds, each time someone declared "the bottom is in," and the third broke below the previous low. That does not prove there will be a fourth or fifth wave, but its shape is the same as the opening of the three histories above.

The backstops themselves can blow up​

So far we have looked at backstops that cannot hold. There is another case fewer people think about: the backstop itself becomes the point of rupture. The past two decades offer at least three examples.

Taiwan's National Stabilization Fund, 2000. The Fund's first intervention came in March 2000, during Taiwan's first transfer of power between parties. It put in about NT$54 billion over five days and eventually lost about NT$50 billion — its only loss to date. All eight interventions since then have closed at a profit. That record of one loss and eight wins shows two things: a backstop can go in at the wrong time and lose heavily; and after eight straight wins, the market has learned that "the Stabilization Fund is buying" means "buy" — exactly the moral hazard described earlier. The better a backstop's track record, the more heavily people bet on it; if it loses next time, the Fund will not be the only one losing.

Bond ETFs, March 2020. ETF money has been the most important "shock absorber" of the past decade, and its promise is that you can sell at any time. But being able to sell the ETF at any time does not mean the assets underneath can be sold at any time. During the worst days of the pandemic panic, the largest US investment-grade corporate bond ETF (LQD) traded at a discount of about 5 percent to its net asset value; on 20 March alone, more than thirty bond ETFs traded at discounts of 5 percent or more. The market only steadied after the Federal Reserve announced on 23 March that it would buy corporate bonds and related ETFs directly. In other words, the first line of shock absorbers needed a second line to rescue it.

UK pension funds, September 2022. This example deserves the closest attention from pension members in Hong Kong and Taiwan. Many UK defined-benefit pension schemes used liability-driven investment (LDI) strategies, holding UK government bonds (gilts) with leverage. Pension funds are supposed to be the most stable, least panic-prone buyers in the market. But after the government announced a tax-cutting budget, gilts plunged, and these leveraged positions faced collateral calls. The only way to raise cash was to sell gilts; the more they sold, the further prices fell, and the further prices fell, the more they had to sell. On 28 September the Bank of England announced it would buy up to £65 billion of gilts. UK inflation was above 10 percent at the time, so the central bank was raising rates to fight inflation with one hand while buying bonds to rescue pension funds with the other. The "first premise" from the previous section has already had one full demonstration here.

All three examples point to the same thing: risk absorbed by the backstops does not disappear; it concentrates on the backstops. The more successful the backstop, the more it absorbs, and when something goes wrong, what goes wrong is the centre of the market.

In today's AI supply chain, there is a fourth form. The second article described the circular guarantees: Nvidia provided a backstop of about US$6.3 billion for CoreWeave's computing capacity and has also invested in OpenAI; AMD's warrants to OpenAI vest in full only if AMD's share price reaches US$600; Google has guaranteed Anthropic's computing commitments. In normal times these arrangements prop each other up; when trouble comes, they drag each other down. If one customer cannot pay, the guarantor has to pay out; when the guarantor's share price falls, the capital it uses to back the guarantee shrinks at the same time. When Nvidia cannot hold, AMD cannot hold either, because they are betting on the same story.

Scorecard: which conditions have appeared​

Five of the ten are present. These five have one thing in common: they are all "fuel." They pile up slowly and do not ignite on their own. The five not yet present are "transmission": ammunition running out, money flowing back out, earnings deteriorating, forced selling, credit stress spreading. They decide whether the fire spreads.

So my judgment today is: the structure is already dangerous, but transmission has not begun. That also explains why the market can fall so deeply and still be "just a correction." Readers do not need to believe me. They only need to come back to this table once a quarter: when the bottom five rows start turning into "Present" one by one, that is when the word "correction" is about to stop working.

What would prove me wrong​

A judgment that cannot be proven wrong is no different from empty talk. So let me be clear: if any of the following sets of circumstances comes about, I am wrong.

Inflation falls, and the central bank stops without a recession. If US inflation falls back below 3 percent, the Fed stops hiking or even cuts, the 10-year yield comes down, and employment and consumption hold up, then the premise that "central banks can print" is back, and the backstops regain their strength.

AI revenue catches up with its commitments. What circular guarantees fear most is revenue that cannot cover contracts. If growth like Anthropic's — third-quarter revenue of about US$17 billion, up about 47 percent on the previous quarter (down from about 144 percent the quarter before, so growth is already slowing) — can be sustained, and the free cash flow of the big cloud companies turns positive again, those guarantees will never be called, and the "circularity" described in the second article will turn out to have been an aggressive but successful way of financing.

The fuel recedes on its own. If foreign money returns to Taiwan stocks, off-balance-sheet debt gradually moves back onto balance sheets, and valuations return to reasonable levels through earnings growth rather than falling prices, then the top five rows of the scorecard will turn back to "Not yet" one by one. Risk being digested without a crash also means I was wrong.

There is also a clarification in the other direction. If the market crashes tomorrow because of a war or some entirely unrelated event, that does not make me right either. This article describes a specific mechanism: the premises of the backstops fail, and deferred losses are released in several waves. Whether I am right depends on whether that mechanism is at work, not on how much the index falls on any given day.

Conclusion: building codes are for your own house​

Back to the earthquake at the start. The point of knowing which buildings will collapse is not to guess the date; it is to make sure you are not living in one of them. For ordinary investors, that comes down to roughly four things:

  1. Do not buy the dip with leverage. Margin, futures and leveraged ETFs can pay off in a single-wave decline, but in a multi-wave decline they will get you liquidated in the second wave. You do not need to guess the bottom right; you only need to still be standing when you guess wrong.
  2. Open your MPF, labor-pension self-directed account or monthly savings plan and look at what it actually holds. Many people think they are diversified, but the top ten holdings of their three funds may be the same companies.
  3. If you are close to retirement, do not wait until prices are low to switch into conservative funds. Switching at a low turns paper losses into real ones. If you need to switch, do it early and in stages, not all at once in a panic.
  4. Keep some cash you will not need to touch. What really hurts people in a multi-wave decline is being forced to sell in the second or third wave. Those with cash can wait; those without can only sell.

Steve Eisman, one of the real-life figures behind The Big Short, said in a recent interview that he does not predict crashes, but he has hedged about 70 percent of his portfolio, because that way he can sleep at night. A man who made a fortune betting against subprime is offering today not a date but a position. That is the most practical thing these three articles have to say.

My family elders left me one phrase: when the profit looks too big, you may not even get your capital back. However large the paper profit, money you cannot collect is not money. Today's market is full of paper numbers: AI contracts not yet booked, guarantees not yet called, MPF balances not yet realised. They are not necessarily fake, but until they are cashed, none of them should be treated as cash.

That is where the three articles end. The first said crashes have been defined as "corrections"; the second said the losses have been moved into pensions and footnotes; this one says the backstops have deferred the risk. Put together, they are one sentence: crashes have not disappeared; they have been renamed, placed on other people's shoulders, and postponed. Postponed is not cancelled.

Back to that line in the group chat at the start of the first article: "Guessing costs nothing." Three articles later, I still have not given a date. Guessing a date really does cost nothing. What takes effort is writing the conditions down one by one, stating the date the data runs to, and letting everyone come back each quarter to check the numbers — against me as well.
