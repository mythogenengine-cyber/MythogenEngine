Jensen Huang Is Gone, the Guarantees Remain: Nvidia's Valuation Counts Earnings, Not the Money It Has Promised to Pay

@Mythogen Engine · Nov 16, 2026

The market is asking who succeeds him. No one is asking who pays​

On November 12, 2026, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO and director, effective immediately, with CFO Colette Kress stepping in as interim CEO. For the next day, the discussion centred on three things: who will take the job permanently, whether a Bezos- or Gates-style "affair rally" will follow, and whether a forward P/E of about 14 counts as cheap.

The three share one feature. They all ask how much Nvidia can still earn. None asks how much money it has already promised to pay out, when that falls due, and who will pay it.

The few comments that mention debt treat it only as a mood gauge: CDS spreads widening, junk-bond spreads widening, borrowing getting dearer. That is a flow question: will new money keep coming in? The stock question, how the money already guaranteed, already extended on credit and already invested gets collected, is left almost untouched.

This article does one thing. It takes Nvidia's most recent quarterly report (second quarter of fiscal 2027, May to July 2026) and lays out, item by item, what the company must pay, what it has guaranteed and what it is owed, to see what a founder leaves behind when he goes.

Three tables: $530.5 billion of commitments and guarantees​

Nvidia's 10-Q for the period ended July 26, 2026 lists three sets of figures: $366.0 billion of general commitments, $56.0 billion of additional commitments, and guarantees with a maximum gross exposure of $108.5 billion. Together they come to about $530.5 billion. Most of this is not debt on the balance sheet. It is payment obligations already signed, triggered when conditions are met.

The supply commitments in the first set are normal for a chip company, and some contracts can still be cancelled or adjusted before orders are firm. What stands out is the jump from $119.0 billion to $279.0 billion in a single quarter. What really ties Nvidia's fate to its customers is the other two sets. If customers can pay, these figures never turn into cash outlays. If they cannot, it is Nvidia's turn.

Below, "paying it back" is split into three ledgers of different kinds.

Ledger one: guarantees. If the customer cannot pay, Nvidia does​

The largest is the Ohio guarantee signed in August 2026. As the 10-Q describes it, Nvidia provides credit support, capped at $105.0 billion, for a data center of about 4.25 GW that SB Energy is building in Pike County and leasing to OpenAI. The guarantee covers only part of the rent and power payments. It does not cover the full cost of the site or all of the tenant's obligations.

Its time structure is the key:

  • It is built in nine phases, each taking effect when that phase's lease begins, with the first expected in fiscal 2029.
  • Each phase's lease runs 20 years, and exposure declines as OpenAI pays rent.
  • The guarantee ends only when OpenAI obtains a "satisfactory credit rating" or the lease term expires.
  • In exchange, the site uses only Nvidia AI infrastructure, and Nvidia may at its discretion provide credit support for about 3.8 GW more.

In fairness, not one dollar of this guarantee is in effect today, and $105.0 billion is a cap, not an expected loss. But its shape deserves a clear look. It begins to take effect only from around 2028, each phase must then hold for twenty years, and the last phase will expire in the 2040s. Huang was CEO for 33 years. The guarantee he signed will outlive his tenure.

The second kind is the AI cloud agreements, $36.0 billion in total. Neoclouds buy Nvidia's equipment, and Nvidia in turn commits to buy their cloud services, typically for six years. One sentence in the terms matters: these AI clouds "can unilaterally stop providing to us" and resell the capacity to a third party that pays more. When the market is good, the compute goes to someone else. When it is bad, Nvidia's commitment is the floor. The upside goes to the cloud; the downside goes to Nvidia. The $6.3 billion backstop order signed with CoreWeave in September 2025 has the same shape: if the compute cannot be rented out, Nvidia must buy the remainder, until April 13, 2032.

Why make these guarantees at all? Nvidia gives the reason itself in the same quarterly report: AI clouds and AI model companies have real demand but "currently lack the ability to secure long-term infrastructure contracts and investment-grade financing capacity." The guarantees exist precisely because the borrowers cannot borrow on their own.

Ledger two: GPU-backed debt. The collateral falls faster than the debt​

The second ledger is not on Nvidia's books. It is on its customers' books, but the collateral is Nvidia's chips. Take CoreWeave, the largest neocloud. At the end of March 2026 it had $11.8 billion outstanding under delayed draw term loans (DDTL), secured by the assets and customer payments under its contracts, plus $6.4 billion of notes due in 2030 and 2031.

The thing to watch is tenor. CoreWeave's $2.6 billion DDTL 5.5, signed in August 2026, matures on September 1, 2031, about five years out. The customer contracts beneath it average only about three years. The company itself says its earlier loans were all supported by contracts covering the full life of the debt, and that this one is the first that is not. The two years in between depend on renewal or re-letting: a bet that three-year-old GPUs can still be rented out, at rents still high enough to service the debt. Nvidia's own product cadence is one generation a year.

The market has already put a price on that two-year gap. DDTL 5.5 pays SOFR plus 5.5 percentage points, after first being marketed at 4.25 to 4.5 points. The $8.5 billion loan of March the same year, with stronger contract support, pays SOFR plus 2.25. CNBC reports that yields on CoreWeave's bonds due 2031 and 2032 have passed 15%. Goldman Sachs estimates CoreWeave's debt could approach $100 billion by 2028, with free cash flow negative until at least 2028. That estimate assumes GPUs remain usable for about 4.5 years.

The books show a different number again. Seven large AI cloud and technology companies set the depreciation life of servers or GPUs at up to six years. Amazon, citing AI, shortened some equipment to five years, while Meta and CoreWeave lengthened theirs. The longer the depreciation life, the better each year's profit looks. But debt is repaid in cash, not in depreciation.

What brings this back to Nvidia is the financing platform of August 2026. Nvidia signed a memorandum of understanding with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, aiming to raise more than $500 billion of third-party money for AI infrastructure. Huang said Nvidia could choose to backstop up to $125 billion of it, about a quarter, and described the chips as assets that generate revenue. A memorandum is not a loan disbursed. But it shows the direction: the company that makes the collateral is also vouching for the collateral's value.

Ledger three: money already booked, not yet collected​

The third ledger has already happened, and it is written in the cash flow statement. In the second quarter of fiscal 2027, Nvidia reported revenue of $96.2 billion and net income of $59.7 billion, yet operating cash flow of only $24.1 billion, half the previous quarter's.

The largest source of the gap is accounts receivable. Receivables rose from $38.5 billion at the end of January to $63.1 billion at the end of July, and days sales outstanding stretched from 45 to 60. Nvidia's explanation: for large, multi-quarter orders from certain investment-grade customers, it offers payment terms of 90 days to one year. Five direct customers account for about 70% of receivables. The report's wording is also worth reading: these financing arrangements "will continue to affect the timing of our operating cash flows." In other words, this is not a one-off.

The other source is equity investments. Nvidia's holdings of public and private equity rose from about $35.1 billion at the end of January to about $93.9 billion at the end of July, and $7.8 billion of second-quarter net income was non-cash gains on equity securities. Most of this money is invested in its own customers. It does not have to be repaid, but it does not turn back into cash by itself either. Its value depends on the customers' next valuation round, and much of what customers raise in that round will be spent on Nvidia's chips.

The practice has continued since the quarter closed. Anthropic's prospectus, updated on November 10, shows that Nvidia agreed to buy about $10 billion of non-voting Class N stock at the IPO price, the same amount as Google. The IPO is expected to price on November 18 and list on the 19th. Whether this sum is already counted in the $25.0 billion of equity investment commitments cannot be determined, because the quarterly report runs only to July.

Fairness is due here. The extended payment terms go to investment-grade customers, bad-debt risk is low, and a delay is not a default. But it changes one thing: a time lag has opened between profit and cash, and Nvidia gave that lag voluntarily. It used its own balance sheet to give customers extra room to take delivery first and pay later. A P/E ratio uses earnings, and those earnings already include money not yet collected and paper gains on stakes in customers.

The person leaves. The contracts stay​

The most common line since the resignation is this: without Huang, Nvidia may be less aggressive about putting money into its customers. That may be right, but it speaks only of new money. A new CEO can stop signing. She cannot withdraw what is already signed:

  • $279.0 billion of supply commitments, about $179.0 billion payable within the next eighteen months;
  • $25.0 billion of equity investment commitments, $18.0 billion of it in the remainder of this fiscal year;
  • $36.0 billion of AI cloud backstops, typically six years;
  • a $108.5 billion cap on guarantees, mostly Ohio, at 20 years per phase;
  • $63.1 billion of receivables, some on terms as long as a year.

The person taking over these ledgers is the one who questioned them internally. According to a Wall Street Journal report of November 13, Kress pressed on guarantees of this kind at an internal meeting this summer: how the deals were chosen, how the guarantees were valued, how the return was calculated. She also helped cut a proposed commitment to OpenAI in half. Huang interrupted her presentation and described her as "a deer in the headlights." The new CEO will very likely sign less. But of the five items above, she cannot take back a single one.

Worse, pulling back would itself make the old obligations easier to trigger. Neoclouds and model companies need Nvidia's money, guarantees and credit because they cannot borrow at investment grade themselves, as Nvidia wrote in its own quarterly report. If Nvidia stops supplying money, their financing costs rise, their expansion slows and their ability to repay weakens. And once they cannot pay, the first party called on to step in is Nvidia, which signed the backstops and guarantees. Keep paying in, and the risk keeps growing. Stop paying in, and the old risk arrives early. It is a structure with no clean exit.

The bond market had begun repricing before the resignation. According to Barclays' five-year CDS data as of October 28, the chipmaker basket was for the first time more expensive than the hyperscaler basket: Intel 104, AMD 121, Nvidia 125 and Broadcom 168 basis points, all above Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet. By November 6, CNBC reported that Nvidia's CDS had risen 76% in a month to about 139 basis points. IFR reported the same day that this level is closer to that of a double-B company, one notch below investment grade, than to the double-A rating Nvidia actually holds. While the stock market was discussing the P/E ratio, the bond market was already asking who pays.

What a 14x P/E leaves out​

A forward P/E of 14 uses the earnings forecast for the coming year. Those earnings may be real, and the orders for 2027 may already be largely settled. But a multiple that looks only at earnings cannot answer two questions: how much of those earnings will come back as cash, and how many obligations hang behind them that will not be released until 2032, or even the 2040s.

Most discussion of Huang's departure asks whether Nvidia can still earn without him. The more practical question is this: the ledgers he signed on the strength of his own relationships and nerve are now taken over by a CFO, serving as interim CEO, who questioned those guarantees internally. Can the customers pay? Money earned on the books and money actually collected are two different things.

What is worth watching next is not the share price but these numbers:

  1. The third-quarter results and earnings call after the US close on November 17 (early morning of the 18th in Taipei): Kress's first account, as interim CEO, of her stance on the guarantees and the OpenAI commitment. The commitments table has to wait for the 10-Q that follows.
  2. Next quarter's receivables and days sales outstanding: do they fall back or keep stretching?
  3. The ratio of operating cash flow to net income, which was about 40% in the second quarter.
  4. Whether the commitments and guarantees tables grow further or start to shrink.
  5. Bond yields, renewal rates and rental prices at CoreWeave and the other neoclouds.
  6. Whether OpenAI can obtain an investment-grade credit rating, which is the release condition for the Ohio guarantee.
