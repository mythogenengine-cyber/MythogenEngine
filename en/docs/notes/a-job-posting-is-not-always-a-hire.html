A Job Posting Is Not Always a Hire: How Restructuring Merges Several Roles into One and Makes Everyone Reapply

Nov 17, 2026 · @Mythogen Engine

How to read this piece​

This is an estimate piece: public numbers, plus practices I have seen, plus my inference connecting the two. The label at the start of a paragraph says what kind of content it is.

  • [Data] Figures from public reports, with source and limits.
  • [Witnessed] Things I saw while employed. The documents will never be public and I cannot produce them, so no company or date is named.
  • [Inference] My reasoning connecting the two. It may be wrong.

What I don't know is listed in section 4.

1. Openings are up, hiring is down​

[Data] In tech, job openings and actual hires have moved in opposite directions over the past two years. Lenny Rachitsky's job market report, published on 10 November 2026, uses two datasets: TrueUp tracks openings at more than 9,000 tech companies worldwide, and Live Data Technologies tracks actual hires in the US.

RoleOpeningsHires
Software engineering+44% since Jan 2024−17% over the same period
Product management+56% since early 2023−4% vs Jan 2024

The report's headline says PMs are winning. Its own numbers show hiring for all five core roles flat or down, with PM falling least: about 34,000 US hires in 2025, 35% fewer than in 2022. Software engineer hires fell 45% over the same span.

The same report has two more numbers. Front-end engineering openings fell 51% in a year. The ratio of open PM roles to open design roles rose from 1.9 in early 2023 to 3.8.

These numbers have two limits. Openings are global; hires are US only. Hires count only people who came from another employer, so internal moves are not counted.

[Data] Other sources point the same way, but they do not agree with one another.

  • Revelio Labs matched postings to hires within six months: hires per posting were 0.75 in 2018 and below 0.5 in 2023.
  • Hiring platform Greenhouse classifies 18–22% of postings on its platform as ghost jobs, and the share stays in that range every quarter (source).
  • The US Bureau of Labor Statistics puts openings to hires at about 1.4 to 1, lower than the 1.8 to 1 of the Great Resignation (cited by TechNews).
  • In a Resume Builder survey, 40% of hiring managers said their company had posted a fake opening. The survey was completed by 649 people and comes from a résumé tool vendor (sample notes).

So only one thing can be said: the rate at which openings turn into hires is falling. "Fake openings are increasing" cannot be proven, because fake is a matter of intent.

Lenny reads this gap as great hires getting harder to land. Below is another reading.

2. What I have seen​

[Witnessed] The practice I saw: the company issued performance improvement plans in large numbers while opening new restructured roles, told everyone to apply, and ended up with fewer people.

  • As departments shrank, performance improvement plans (PIPs) went out in large numbers. At the same time, the company opened a batch of roles and called them transfer opportunities.
  • These roles came out of restructuring. Responsibilities and functions were added, and the nature of the work changed.
  • A PM-type role could become a grab-bag: the authority to decide, without accountability for results.
  • Applicants were not split into internal and external. Everyone interviewed and competed together.
  • When the competition was over, the outcome was layoffs and transfers, not added headcount.

That is what I saw. I don't have the decision process or the documents, so this section does not say why the company did it.

3. Connecting the two​

[Inference] If this practice is not limited to a few companies, it alone would produce "openings up, hiring down."

  1. Adding responsibilities and functions to one role amounts to merging work that used to be separate. Each restructuring posts a batch of new openings while more old seats disappear.
  2. The new roles are also posted externally, and platforms that track openings count them. If insiders end up in them, external hires are zero, and the report's hiring figures don't count internal moves.
  3. If merged roles carry a PM title, both PM openings and PM's share of hiring go up. The report says front-end openings halved, specialist silos collapsed, and smaller teams each own more. Seen from inside a company, that may be the same thing.
  4. For the company, this turns "you were laid off" into "you didn't win the new role", placing the reason for leaving on the individual.

All four are inference. Point 4 is about motive, and motive can't be proven.

In an earlier piece, "The JD Looks Great on Paper — The Reality of the 'Forward Deployed Engineer' Role", I wrote about the gap between job descriptions and the actual work. This adds one possible cause: a job description reads rich because several roles were merged into one.

4. What I don't know​

I have no answer to these four.

  • How common it is. I have only seen the places I worked.
  • How much it explains. There are no numbers for what share of the gap between openings and hires comes from restructured roles.
  • The role of external applicants. How many get hired in these rounds, and what the company gains from interviewing them, I can't write with confidence.
  • Taiwan. All the numbers above are from the US. I found no local data.

5. Three questions to ask​

People told they can apply for a new role while employed, and outsiders who see such openings, can ask three things first.

  1. How many old roles was this job description merged from? Where did the people who did that work go?
  2. Are the things this role controls the same things it is accountable for?
  3. Has the company restructured recently? Are insiders applying for this role at the same time?

A job description that covers everything is itself a visible signal.

Before reading any job market report, ask one question: does this number measure posted openings, or people actually hired?
