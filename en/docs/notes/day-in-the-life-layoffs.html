How a Day-in-the-Life Video Became a Reason for Layoffs: People Without Capital Punishing People Without Capital

Snatching Food from a Beggar's Bowl series · @Mythogen Engine · Nov 21, 2026

1. Beggars Only Envy Beggars​

In The Conquest of Happiness (1930), Bertrand Russell gives a whole chapter to envy. One line from it: "Beggars do not envy millionaires, though of course they will envy other beggars who are more successful." His reasoning is that we do not envy good fortune we see as hopelessly out of reach. Envy needs proximity, close enough that it feels like it could have been us.

Nearly a century later, the line came true again at Microsoft. On 26 October 2026, Microsoft announced a Voluntary Exit Offer to about 60,000 eligible employees in the United States, Canada, India and the United Kingdom. That is roughly 27% of its 223,000 full-time staff. The same month, Microsoft's share price hit an all-time high.

As the news spread, an old video from 2025 resurfaced. A 24-year-old engineer at Microsoft's office in Hyderabad, India, had filmed a day at work: breakfast, dessert, snacks, games. Reposted under captions like "No wonder Microsoft is cutting 60,000", it went viral again, and the outrage drowned out everything else.

The person being attacked was a young engineer not long into her job. Few people asked why a company at a record share price, spending heavily on AI, wanted a quarter of its workforce to leave "voluntarily". Once again, the beggars turned their eyes to the bowl next door.

2. What the Video Showed, and What It Didn't​

The engineer in the video is Mansi Singhal. In the clip she says she usually arrives at 9 a.m. and leaves at 6:30 p.m. Reports describe it consistently: a 45-minute breakfast, dessert, a gaming session and several snack runs. After it went viral, she posted that she tends to show the "good side" of developer life, and that the reality is often messier and harder.

On some Chinese-language channels, the story became a different version: gym and shower at nine, work starting at ten-thirty, out the door at 4:20 p.m. I could not find 4:20 in any mainstream report. Even by these channels' own count, she spent six hours at her desk. By her own account, she was in the office for nine and a half hours.

What matters more is what the camera left out. Nobody wants to watch someone write code, and company material cannot be filmed. Crunch before a delivery, midnight on-call, meetings across time zones with headquarters: none of it fits in a reel a few dozen seconds long. The day-in-the-life genre exists to show the good side, because only the good side gets watched.

Anyone who has worked on projects knows work has peak and slack seasons. Ten-plus-hour days in the peak, a looser pace in the slack: it is just balance. But the viewer's ruler measures only one thing, whether hardship is visible. Output is invisible; hardship is visible. So visible hardship gets treated as value.

3. Perks Are Designed to Keep You In​

Microsoft's development centre in Hyderabad covers 54 acres. Reported facilities include a 24-hour multi-cuisine cafeteria, a gym, a nap room, a pharmacy, round-the-clock ambulance service and air-conditioned shuttle buses with Wi-Fi.

These are not gifts to employees; they are a design. A cafeteria open around the clock means dinner can be eaten at the office. Wi-Fi on the shuttle turns the commute into working time. A nap room means you need not go home to sleep. Each facility removes one more reason to leave the building.

So the question should not be how leisurely she was. It should run the other way: after the gym, how late did she work? Were the early-start days left out of the edit? When a company tells you to "treat this place as home", does it mean it wants all your time spent here?

The irony is that these videos began as recruitment ads. In 2024, when Hyderabad employees posted a video showing off their perks, Microsoft's official Microsoft Life account cheered it on. The same kind of video is employer branding in a hiring cycle and evidence for layoffs in a cutting cycle. Viewers see the bait and think they are seeing laziness.

4. Too Hardworking to Sleep​

If the issue really were effort, a hard worker should get a different verdict. In November 2026, the YouTube channel 胡萝卜 ("Carrot") posted a video about a short-drama actress in Hengdian, China's largest film-studio town. Song Nianhuan is 26 and arrived in Hengdian in November 2023, when anyone who wanted roles could get them. This year, as AI short dramas took off, many short-drama companies folded. In May and June combined she worked one day on set; in July, two.

To cover living costs, she set up a stall selling hand-pounded lemon tea. She starts preparing ice at five or six in the evening and packs up at half past one or two in the morning, sometimes as late as half past three. A good night brings in about 280 yuan; a bad summer night, a little over 100. In her most anxious stretch she cried often, and once went a full 24 hours unable to sleep.

This is someone working so hard she barely sleeps. Yet the comments' verdict barely changed. One said she was "genuinely not good enough", or she would have become an AI engineer. Another said "choice matters more than effort". Another asked, "Did she really try her best?" Some said flatly that she was always cut out for a street stall. Hardest of all, at the start of the video she had already passed the same sentence on herself: "It's because I didn't try hard enough, I'm not good enough."

Put the two videos side by side and the structure of the verdict is plain:

  • Not hardworking enough: you deserve to be fired.
  • Working so hard you can't sleep, and still losing: you chose the wrong career, you aren't good enough.
  • Show the good side and you're lazy; show the hardship and you're playing for sympathy: in this video's comments, someone really did say she was "most likely acting".

The verdict is decided in advance; only the reasons change. "Hard work" is not the measure; it is the wording of the sentence. What makes this belief so effective is that the people it condemns believe it too.

This video has no host steering the audience, and its comments are not all verdicts. The most-liked one reads: "A grain of sand from the times, landing on an ordinary person, becomes a mountain collapsing" (140 likes). Others pushed back on "choice matters more than effort", pointing out that before that saying applies, most people are simply lucky or not.

5. The Ticket Sellers​

The people who organise this envy are not beggars; they sell tickets. The Hong Kong channel 屈機TV (WatGei TV) has 610,000 subscribers. On 12 November 2026 it released episode 441 of its show 屈機觀察, titled (in translation) "Microsoft purges Indian office slackers; White House discards them after use and blocks green card applications!" At the time of writing the video had about 300,000 views and 7,501 likes.

Early on, the host, 葉文斌 (Terry), actually says two correct things. First, Microsoft has clarified that this is a buyout employees choose for themselves, not a dismissal targeting any one ethnic group. Second, the public's anger has not been aimed at the decision-makers. He then spends the next nineteen minutes steering that anger toward Indian employees, describing the whole plan as a "strategic reckoning" with Indian networks inside the company.

The video's key claims can be checked one by one against the public record:

Claim in the videoPublic record
"Microsoft's mass layoff of 60,000"60,000 is the number eligible to apply, not the number laid off; employees choose, with a decision due by early December
The engineer started work at 10:30 and left at 4:20 p.m.She says she arrives at 9 and leaves at 6:30; no mainstream report mentions 4:20
Vance said Microsoft laid off 6,000 Americans while applying for over 2,200 H-1BsVance's words were 6,000 laid off while benefiting from 6,300 H-1B visas and nearly 3,000 green cards; CNBC's analysis found two-thirds of Microsoft's H-1B approvals went to existing employees; Microsoft says H-1B holders also lost their roles
The Labor Department suspended Microsoft and others from green card applicationsTrue: on 8 October it suspended Microsoft, Adobe and six IT firms from PERM labour certification; existing H-1Bs are unaffected
India-born applicants hold over 70% of H-1Bs, China 11.7%True (fiscal 2024); but this is the birthplace of visa holders, not the ethnicity of executives
The Cognizant case proves Indian exclusionThe verdict is real: in October 2024 a jury found the company discriminated against non-Indian staff, and the company said it would appeal; but Cognizant is an outsourcing firm, not Microsoft, and the video's "8.3%" has no source I could find
Indians hold 32% of Silicon Valley VP-and-above roles, Chinese only 4%No source found; a 2021 BBC report put Indian-origin people at about 1% of the US population and 6% of Silicon Valley's workforce

The video misleads mainly through three techniques:

  1. Swapping the number: "60,000 eligible" becomes "60,000 laid off". A plan employees opt into becomes a forced purge.
  2. Mixing true and false: the H-1B share, the PERM suspension and the Cognizant verdict are all true, and they build the scaffolding; the unsourced 32% versus 4% sits where the conclusion goes. Once viewers have verified the first half, they accept the second half with it.
  3. Contradicting itself: the opening concedes that Microsoft has denied targeting any ethnic group; the rest of the video presents the plan as a "strategic reckoning" aimed at Indians. The early clarification makes the show look balanced; the nineteen minutes after it are what viewers take away.

How the tickets are sold is just as clear. The video carries a mid-roll ad for a Chinese herbal remedy, the channel's shop sells mugs, books and cold medicine, and the description links a WhatsApp broadcast group. Anger is traffic, and traffic is ad inventory. The host is not a beggar; he is the one at the edge of the square selling tickets to watch the beggars fight.

The comment section is the result. Sorted by "Top", the most-liked comments have already left Microsoft behind. One asks why the Hong Kong government still approves so many Indian "talents" (103 likes). One says "the office will finally lose that familiar smell of curry" (46 likes). One reads an Indian father and daughter handing out free meal boxes in Hong Kong, and more South Asian volunteers at charities, as a "hearts-and-minds plot" (55 likes). When a viewer joked that a Microsoft outage that day was Indian engineers "at work", the host's own account (@terry.ymp1101), which thanks supporters one by one in the comments, replied: "Possibly 😂".

Not everyone bought a ticket. One wrote: "When one man rises, his chickens and dogs rise with him. That has nothing to do with race" (1 like). Another noted that locals spend just as long on coffee and chat, and that in the end it is just big companies fleecing the flock (2 likes). Another asked, "Where's your data from?" (0 likes). The doubting voices were always there; nobody gave them likes.

Comparing this with the Hengdian video in the previous section, the difference is stark: there, the most-liked comment talks about the times; here, the most-liked comments talk about ethnicity. Two videos are only two samples and prove nothing in general, but they do show this much: when someone is selling tickets, the audience's judgement is far more one-sided.

In 1919, Lu Xun described this mindset in Random Thoughts No. 65 (translation mine):

The subjects of a tyrant are, as a rule, more tyrannical than the tyrant; the tyrant's tyranny often still cannot satisfy the appetite of his subjects. … The subjects of a tyrant wish only that the tyranny fall on other people's heads. They watch with delight, take "cruelty" as entertainment and "the suffering of others" as a spectacle and a comfort. Their only skill is "escaping".

From among those who "escaped", victims are chosen again, to feed the bloodthirst of the tyrant's subjects, and no one understands. The dead cry "Ah!", and the living rejoice.

Those cheering "well done" in the comments are celebrating their own temporary escape. What they have not considered is that the next victim chosen from among the escaped may be themselves.

6. Recast as an Ethnic Story​

Once it becomes an ethnic question, the structure disappears from the picture. This debate has at least three versions: Indians rise through performance and nepotism; Chinese are diligent and reserved, too principled to self-promote, so they stall; and, often heard in comments and private conversation, Chinese stall because they are suspected of being spies. What the three share is that they turn a problem of systems into a problem of national character.

The gap is real. A 2020 study by Jackson Lu, Richard Nisbett and Michael Morris in PNAS, combining nine studies and 11,030 participants, found that East Asians were less likely than South Asians and whites to reach leadership positions, while South Asians even outperformed whites. Of the 16 Asian CEOs in the S&P 500 in 2017, 13 were South Asian and only 3 East Asian.

But the cause the study found was neither nepotism nor virtue. The gap was consistently explained by cultural differences in assertiveness; prejudice and motivation did not explain it. Silence that reads as steadiness in East Asian cultures reads as lack of confidence in American leadership culture. The authors' conclusion is that this is a problem of cultural fit. The "Chinese are too clean" version repackages that mismatch as a virtue: the audience feels good, and learns nothing.

The spy theory cannot explain the data either. US security concerns about Chinese nationals are real, but the study's "East Asians" include Koreans and Japanese, who stall just the same. The study also found the same gap among US-born East Asians. Americans born and raised in the US are not held back by espionage suspicion.

None of this means hometown favouritism doesn't exist. The Cognizant case is one proven in court: in October 2024, a federal jury found that the outsourcing firm intentionally discriminated against more than 2,000 non-Indian employees between 2013 and 2022. But that is a question of one company, one system and one body of evidence, to be examined company by company; it cannot be inflated into the nature of a people.

Chinese societies have plenty of the same. Hometown associations, clan businesses, alumni circles and dinner-table networking are everyday life in Hong Kong and Taiwan. Calling a huge meeting to show one's importance, racing to report upward, lining up to pick a side: I have seen all of it in Chinese companies. Wherever output is hard to measure, ratings depend on a superior's impression, and layers are so many that the top cannot see the bottom, someone will put on a show. The comment with a single like put it best: when one man rises, his chickens and dogs rise with him, regardless of race.

7. Layoffs Don't Cure Big-Company Disease​

WatGei TV presents the buyout as "clearing out the slackers" and curing big-company disease. That claim has three problems.

First, a voluntary exit tends to drive out the people who have options. The capable, the ones who can find work elsewhere, are the most likely to take the money and go; those who cannot move stay behind. In April 2026 Microsoft offered voluntary retirement to about 8,750 long-serving US staff, and roughly 30% accepted. Who accepts and who refuses is a personal calculation, not a company filter.

Second, bureaucracy follows structure, not headcount. How many approval layers, how many reports, how KPIs are set: none of this changes because people leave. Fewer people with unchanged processes means fewer people producing the same amount of reporting. Everyone fears being next, and the show-making only grows.

Third, innovation needs slack. The looser pace of a slack season is slack, and only with slack do people try new things or attempt work that may not pay off. Strip out all slack as waste, and what is left only maintains the status quo.

To be fair, this buyout was never meant to cure big-company disease. Reports agree that Microsoft is cutting costs while spending heavily on AI. Measured by that yardstick, it may not fail. Layoffs fix a problem on the financial statements, not a problem in the organisation. Companies like to present the former as the latter, and channels like WatGei TV supply the last missing piece: a moral story that makes the layoffs look deserved.

8. Same Premise, New Setting​

The first piece in this series was about a girl carrying beer at Fu Tai Estate in Tuen Mun, Hong Kong. Several channels turned one family into evidence of welfare (CSSA) abuse, on the premise that "if you don't work, you deserve to suffer". This piece rests on exactly the same premise in its white-collar version: if you don't suffer enough, you deserve to be fired.

One is set in a housing estate, the other at Microsoft. The setting can change at any time; the structure never does. The people who decide who goes and who stays are off camera. On camera there are only people without capital, checking whether each other is suffering enough. Punishing people who don't work hard is, in practice, people without capital punishing people without capital.

And this ruler does not stay on other people. Once a society accepts that "no visible hardship means no claim to resources", the standard climbs upward from the bottom: from welfare families to migrant workers, to imported talent, to white-collar staff, until finally it is held against the very people applauding in the comments today.

Russell explains why envy aims only at the beggar nearby; Lu Xun explains why others' suffering becomes the onlookers' entertainment. Only one role is missing in between: the person who joins the two together and sells tickets at the edge of the square. The next time you see a headline like "No wonder they're laying people off", it may be worth asking first: why are the people who should really be questioned never in the picture?
