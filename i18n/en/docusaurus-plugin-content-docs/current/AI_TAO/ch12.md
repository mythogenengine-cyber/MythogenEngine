# Chapter 12: Grasping the Meaning, Releasing the Image — Word, Image, Meaning

## "Consciousness" Is a Trap

Chapter 11's conclusion was: tools can be replicated; consciousness cannot. But this conclusion has a problem of its own — what is "consciousness"?

The word is a trap, because every person who hears it pictures something different.

When the AI industry speaks of consciousness, it means "does the machine have subjective experience?" — Anthropic is testing whether models show signs of suffering, Google DeepMind has commissioned philosophers to study machine consciousness. The question they ask is ontological: is AI a "sentient being"?

When the general public says consciousness, they mean "are you paying attention?" — environmental consciousness, safety consciousness, problem consciousness. This usage is shallow, essentially equivalent to "awareness."

The consciousness this book speaks of is neither of these.

It refers to something more elemental: the judgement and sense of direction that settle within a person after they have lived, suffered, and thought — it determines what you choose to say, why you say it, and what is not worth saying. Chapter 10 used the "five layers of scarcity" to locate it: direction, judgement, world-model, trust, meaning. What this chapter sets out to do is open up its internal structure.

Over two thousand years ago, a framework already deconstructed the layers of "expression" and "understanding" to their extremes. It comes not from Western philosophy, not from cognitive science, but from the *I Ching*.

## Word, Image, Meaning

The expressive system of the *I Ching* can be broken into three layers: word (*yan*), image (*xiang*), and meaning (*yi*).

Meaning is the highest-density compression. It is the patterns, feelings, and experiences of heaven, earth, and the human world, compressed into a state approaching nothingness. You know it is there, but when you reach out to grasp it, you cannot. It is like the "thing" that remains in your mind after you have finished a book or lived through an experience — not specific words, not specific images, but a kind of understanding, a kind of judgement, a sense of direction.

Image is the transformation layer. Meaning is too ethereal; it cannot be transmitted directly to another person. So it must be transformed into something tangible — metaphor, imagery, story, scene. Image is meaning having found a body, allowing it to be felt, touched, understood. Image is the most aesthetically rich of the three, because it simultaneously carries the depth of meaning and the tangibility of word.

Word is the outermost layer of output. It records the image, turning it into text and language that can be transmitted, communicated, and precisely replicated. The zenith of word is legal statutes and mathematical formulas — precise, unambiguous, carrying the least information yet with the smallest deviation.

The relationship among the three is not a staircase but a flow. Meaning can be transformed into image; image can be transformed into word. And in reverse: reading word can yield image; grasping image, one can release word; grasping meaning, one can release image. This is what Zhuangzi meant by "having caught the fish, forget the trap," and what Wang Bi meant by "grasping the meaning, release the image; grasping the image, release the word" — once the tool or vessel has fulfilled its mission, it can be set down, because you have already taken hold of what it carried.

This framework sounds like literary theory. It is not. It is a disassembly tool, applicable to every problem this book has encountered — why tautologies are empty, why AI-generated text fails to earn trust, why education cannot teach judgement, why corporate rhetoric works. All can be repositioned through the three layers of word, image, and meaning.

## The Weight of Fourteen Characters

First, let us see what "meaning" looks like when compressed to its absolute limit.

"Heaven moves with vigour; the noble person ceaselessly strives for self-improvement. Earth's nature is receptive; the noble person carries all things with generous virtue."

These fourteen characters are a specimen of meaning.

Break open any single character, and you could write a dissertation. But taken together, you need no academic background to feel their weight — because your lived experience automatically verifies them. You have been through moments requiring self-strengthening; you have encountered situations calling for generous virtue. These characters are not telling you something new; they are helping you compress experience you already possess.

Moreover, these fourteen characters carry a position. You can argue against them — "What if you strive to the utmost and still fail?" "What if generous virtue is exploited?" They do not avoid being challenged, because what they express is a judgement that carries risk, not a safe piece of platitude.

This is the fundamental difference between "meaning" and "tautology."

Chapter 11 analysed the writing formula of a certain bestselling author: "If you don't want to break up, you won't break up." "Those who leave were always going to leave." These sentences are always true — but the cost of always being true is that they say nothing. You cannot even refute them, because they never opened their mouth in the first place.

Both are sparse in wording; both contain white space. But "Heaven moves with vigour; the noble person ceaselessly strives for self-improvement" is white space *after* compression — behind every character lie millennia of human experience. "If you don't want to break up, you won't break up" is white space where there was never anything — it mimics the sparseness of poetry in form, but inside it is empty.

Someone once defended that bestselling author: what he writes is actually a kind of poetry — have you ever seen poetry crammed dense across an entire book?

This defence itself precisely exposes the problem. The white space of poetry is white space after compression — every gap has weight, because what the poet chose not to say matters as much as what they chose to say. But the white space of tautology is not compression; it is dilution. You would not call a glass of plain water an espresso, even if their cups are equally small.

## The Meaning of Heaven and Earth, the Meaning of the Human World

The *I Ching* uses heaven and earth as metaphors for being human. The *Tao Te Ching* uses heaven and earth as metaphors for being human. The *Doctrine of the Mean* says "Heaven is lofty and luminous; Earth is vast and substantial" — also using heaven and earth as metaphors for being human. Three different systems of thought, different authors, different eras, all employing the same metaphorical framework.

This is not coincidence. Heaven and earth are the largest shared frame of reference in human experience — every person who has ever lived has seen the sky and stood upon the ground. Using heaven and earth as "image" to transform abstract "meaning" into something everyone can feel produces the least ambiguity and the greatest resonance. This is the standard technique of the Four Books and Five Classics: begin with the most stable, least controversial patterns.

But human affairs are entirely different.

Ethics is multi-directional — filial duty and loyalty can conflict; righteousness and sentiment can contradict. Constraints are conditional — the same rule can be protection in one context and oppression in another. Emotions are fluid — the same person's feelings at different moments can be completely opposite. Good and evil are entangled — a single action can spring simultaneously from goodness and selfishness. Calculation operates on multiple levels — beneath the surface intent may lie several more layers of true motive.

Compressing these things into "meaning" is orders of magnitude harder than compressing the patterns of heaven and earth. Because the patterns of heaven and earth can be stated in a single sentence, but if you state a pattern of the human world in a single sentence, someone can always find a counter-example — because human complexity inherently resists compression.

This is why writing a novel is harder than writing an aphorism. An aphorism compresses the meaning of heaven and earth — constant patterns, reducible to fourteen characters. A novel compresses the meaning of the human world — good and evil entangled, emotions flowing, motives layered — you need a hundred thousand words, even several hundred thousand, to compress the process of a person being crushed by a system and standing up again into a story from which a reader can "grasp the meaning."

This also explains why the corporate culture problem dismantled in Chapter 9 is so hard to resolve. Every decision inside a company is a battlefield of human-world meaning — consensus costs, struggles over narrative power, post-hoc attribution — every layer tangled with good and evil, with multi-directional ethical conflicts. Compressing all of this with a single KPI is like using tautology to summarise the human world — formally clean, substantively capturing nothing.

## Taiji — The Gradient Between Zero and One

If you can see through the Taiji, it is really just two fish — zero and one — chasing each other.

But between zero and one there is no switch. There is 0.1, 0.5, 0.001, 0.999. This process of gradation is the truth hidden within the Taiji. Yin contains yang; yang contains yin — in the Taiji diagram, the black fish's eye is white, the white fish's eye is black. No absolute zero, no absolute one. At the extreme of all things, a thread remains.

Within good lie the seeds of evil; within evil lies the possibility of good. Within oppression there is an element of protection; within freedom there is the risk of licence. This is not ambiguity, not fence-sitting — this is the true structure of human-world meaning. It refuses to be simplified into black-and-white judgement, because reality itself is not black and white.

This forms a fundamental contrast with how the AI industry discusses "whether AI has consciousness."

They ask a binary question: has consciousness or not? Yes or no? But the Taiji framework tells you: consciousness is not yes or no — it is how much, how deep, how compressed. How much a person has lived through, thought about, suffered — the density of their consciousness sits at a specific point on that spectrum. Not has or has not, but thick or thin.

This framework of gradation can also recalibrate the judgements of earlier chapters. The education problem dismantled in Chapter 7 is fundamentally a problem of "the thickness of meaning" — operations-oriented education trains only the "word" layer, skipping the observation of "image" and the compression of "meaning," so students graduate knowing how to operate tools but not knowing when tools should not be used. The prose illusion dismantled in Chapter 8 mistakes the fluency of "word" for the presence of "meaning." The corporate rhetoric dismantled in Chapter 9 uses elaborate "image" (consensus language, cultural-values posters, mission statements) to cover the absence of "meaning." All three problems share the same structure: word and image are present, but meaning is absent.

## What AI Can and Cannot Do

Using the word-image-meaning framework to re-examine AI, many things become clear.

At the "word" layer, AI is nearly invincible. Grammar correct, structure complete, diction appropriate, formatting standard. Legal documents, technical documentation, business emails — these zenith forms of "word" are already done better by AI than by most humans. This is why "prose" as a threshold is disappearing: the production cost of "word" has reached zero.

At the "image" layer, AI can imitate shapes. It can generate metaphors, tell stories, create vivid scenes. Given sufficient context and examples, the "image" it generates can even seem quite moving. But look closely, and you will find its images are collages — it extracts vast numbers of metaphorical patterns from training data, then assembles a new one according to the statistically optimal solution. The shape is right, but the image did not grow from a specific "meaning"; it was pieced together from a pattern library.

At the "meaning" layer, AI cannot even touch the edge. Because meaning is not information, not pattern, not statistical optimum. Meaning is the chemical reaction, emotional turbulence, and cognitive reorganisation triggered within a person after living through an experience, which ultimately settle into a judgement, a direction, an "I know what is important." This process requires a body, a span of time, a set of unreplicable experiences. AI has none of these.

The Schopenhauer consolation text from the Prologue is a perfect specimen of "word and image present, meaning absent." Disassemble it with the word-image-meaning framework: its "word" is fluent — grammar correct, diction appropriate, structure complete, even carrying a certain literary cadence. Its "image" has shape — Schopenhauer's name provides philosophical imagery; "a clarity that has been polished" creates a visual scene; "the answer you have been searching for" suggests an inner journey. Every shape is in place. But its "meaning" is zero. Not a single sentence comes from a specific person's specific experience. Not a single judgement bears the risk of being refuted. Its "polished clarity" is not something that actually emerged from someone who was truly polished — it is a phrase a statistical model assembled based on the co-occurrence frequency of "polish" and "clarity" across vast volumes of text.

Place it beside "Heaven moves with vigour; the noble person ceaselessly strives for self-improvement," and the gap is laid bare. Fourteen characters, every one backed by judgements compressed from millennia of human experience — with a stance, refutable, carrying risk. The Schopenhauer consolation text has dozens of characters, every one backed by emptiness — not the nothingness left after compression, but the nothingness of never having contained anything.

So when a person writes using AI, what is truly happening is: they transform their own "meaning," through AI's capabilities at the "image" and "word" layers, into text a reader can receive. AI is an accelerator of transformation, but the source of "meaning" is always the person.

Conversely, when a person has AI generate an entire article from start to finish, injecting none of their own meaning, that article is an image without meaning — it has the shape of an article, reads smoothly, but inside it is empty. A reader's intuition will sense this emptiness, even if they cannot articulate why.

This also allows us to reposition the bestselling author's problem from Chapter 11. His writing does not lack "prose" — his "word" is actually effective: short sentences, white space, rhythmic cadence all in place. His problem is not that he lacks "image" — he has metaphors, scenes, emotional atmosphere. What he truly lacks is "meaning." His text jumps directly from "word" to the shape of "image," with no "meaning" compressed from lived experience supporting it underneath. So it is always true, and it always says nothing.

## Grasping the Image, Releasing the Word; Grasping the Meaning, Releasing the Image

"Grasping the meaning, release the image; grasping the image, release the word" — this sentence does not say that image and word are unimportant. It says: when you truly understand something (grasping meaning), the imagery that carried it (image) can be set down; when through imagery you have understood the principle (grasping image), the text that recorded it (word) can also be set down.

Setting down is not discarding; it is transcending. You finish a book; you may not remember every sentence, but that book changed the way you see the world — you released the word, but grasped the meaning. You lived through a relationship; the scenes have already blurred, but that experience shaped your understanding of people — you released the image, but grasped the meaning.

This direction can also be reversed. You have a clear judgement in your mind (meaning), but you have not yet found the right metaphor to express it (lacking image), so for the time being you cannot articulate it. Then one day, an experience, a scene, a metaphor suddenly appears and perfectly carries your meaning — that is "meaning finding its image." Then you write it into text (word), and others can understand.

The highest thinking requires meaning — you must be able to directly touch the patterns and essence of things in a state without language, without imagery. The highest expression requires word — because no matter how deep the meaning, if it cannot be transformed into language others can receive, it remains only yours. And image is the bridge between the two — it gives the ethereal meaning a tangible body, and gives precise word a felt warmth. Good image is something you read and immediately know what it says, yet feel it says more than what you read.

AI can produce word; it can assemble image. But it does not know when to release word, when to release image — because it has never "grasped" meaning.

## The Word, Image, and Meaning of This Chapter Itself

This chapter used several images: the heaven-and-earth metaphor, the Taiji gradient, the contrast between espresso and plain water, the weight of fourteen characters, the disassembly of the Schopenhauer consolation text. These concrete images attempt to turn an ethereal framework into something you can touch.

But they are only vessels. If after reading you have memorised these metaphors and scenes and think "the analysis makes sense," yet cannot say what it changed about your own thinking — you have grasped the image but not the meaning. If you forget the specific sentences, the specific metaphors, but the way you look at "AI writing," "the relationship between tools and people," and "what it means to have something to say" has shifted just a little — then you have grasped the meaning.

How much you catch depends on how much you have lived through. This is not a polite formula; it is how "meaning" operates: it can only be perceived by experience of equivalent density.

_Word, image, and meaning are a framework. But the framework itself is not strength — using the framework to see the world, make judgements, and reorganise yourself amidst change: that mode of operation is._
