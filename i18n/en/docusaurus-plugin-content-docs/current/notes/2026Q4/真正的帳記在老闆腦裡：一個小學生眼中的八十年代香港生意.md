# The Real Books Were Kept in the Boss's Head: 1980s Hong Kong Business Through a Primary-School Kid's Eyes

Nov 18, 2026 · @Mythogen Engine

When I was in primary school, I often stood beside the adults and watched them do their sums. This was 1980s Hong Kong, and three things sat on the grown-ups' desks at the same time: a computer, a calculator and an abacus.

A child can't read accounts, but a child can read movements. Which slip went with which tool, which numbers got written down and which didn't — watch long enough and you remember.

Looking back nearly forty years later, those movements were a set of business rules in themselves. Nobody taught me on purpose. I just watched from the side.

## The Abacus for Inside Numbers, the Calculator for Outside Ones

There were calculators by then, and computers, but the adults didn't put their abacuses away. The division of labour was clear, and it wasn't about how big the numbers were. It was about what kind of paper they were on.

Handwritten loose slips, payroll, the profit and loss of a business, all the small odds and ends: those went on the abacus. Contracts, stocks, anything that had to be formally printed or entered into a computer: those went on the computer and the calculator.

As a kid I just found it odd. Only when I grew up did I understand: the first kind were numbers for insiders, the second were numbers to hand to other people. A contract needs the other party's signature; a stock trade comes with a broker's note; either might have to go to a bank or a lawyer. Those had to be neat, printable, kept. Internal numbers never needed to be shown to outsiders. What mattered was that the boss knew them himself.

## Some Numbers Were Never Written Down

The abacus has one thing a calculator doesn't: when you're done, you flick the beads back and no trace is left.

Some numbers, the adults never wrote down after working them out. They kept them in their heads. The amount they could remember was astonishing.

So in that generation's businesses, the real ledger wasn't on paper. Staff, relatives and the accountant could go through every receipt and still not piece together the whole picture, because the few numbers that mattered most were known to the boss alone. Whoever held the real numbers called the shots.

The cost was that when the person left, the numbers left with him. Plenty of family businesses only found out when the next generation took over: figures that didn't add up, accounts of unknown origin, favours owed to who knew whom. Not because the next generation was stupid, but because that ledger had never been written down.

## The Uncle Who Wanted to See Under the Table

Business was easy in the 1980s. Margins were fat and orders plentiful. The people below skimmed a little "tea money" off purchasing and wastage, and most bosses let it go. Chasing it took time and made enemies; it cost more than what was being skimmed.

I remember one uncle in the engineering trade who was different. He badly wanted to understand how the off-the-books numbers in his own company moved: who was taking a cut in the middle, where the money was leaking. At the time I couldn't see why he cared so much.

Later he sold the business, emigrated to America, and we lost touch. When he left, business was still good and buyers were still willing to pay. Looking back, if you want to sell a business, you first have to know what it's really worth; a boss who can't see under his own table can't even name a price.

I later heard he was living an easy life over there, driving himself around Yellowstone, New York, Los Angeles and Miami. He often said there was nothing to do abroad, but he never came back to Asia.

By "nothing to do" he meant no business to run, not no life to live. The boredom was probably exactly the result his abacus had been working toward all those years.

## My Father Taught Me Ratios

The abacus isn't only for adding and subtracting. When my father taught me, he covered multiplication, division, how to work out percentages, even square roots. Sadly I wasn't interested back then, and I've forgotten all of it.

But I remember that what he taught wasn't maths homework. Ratios meant what commission to take or what discount to give for a deal to be worth doing. Division meant how many instalments to collect a sum in, and how much each time. And there was the ratio of spending to income, and how to keep it in check so the cash never ran dry. An abacus can handle all of this. The hard part is doing it against real money every day, and then actually acting on the result.

Outside there were plenty of abacus and mental-arithmetic classes, teaching you to be fast, pass grades, win competitions. But they taught the skill for its own sake, with exercises that had nothing to do with real business. At home, the tool was taught together with judgement, because those numbers decided whether the family ate.

Classes can't teach that, because nobody outside has ever run those numbers with you using their own fortune.

## Having a Business, and Not Having One

In those days there was a type of person who got rich trading stocks. Calling it borrowing to speculate isn't quite right. What they used was the business's own float: money already collected from the last round, money not yet due to go out, and the gap in between went into leverage.

Plenty of people got rich on this kind of leveraged investing; the path itself is nothing unusual. The difference is who walks it. Leverage is leverage, but when a salaried worker takes the same path, the risk he carries is out of all proportion to the boss's.

With a business behind you, the money you can borrow and the money a worker can borrow belong to two different worlds. A worker buys on margin; the moment prices break through, his position is force-sold, and in a crash his job often goes too. A boss, as long as cash is still flowing in from the business, can keep paying interest, doesn't have to dump assets cheap, and can wait for the market to recover. That's why, through several crashes, people with businesses rode out the storms.

So that generation of bosses didn't get rich on short-term trading. They got rich holding long with leverage: the business's cash flow held them up, they weren't forced out when the market fell, and they won when it came back.

You often hear people say they don't have enough capital, so short-term trading is the only way up. But with small capital, big leverage and no cash flow underneath, one drop and you're liquidated, without even the chance to wait. They learned the bosses' leverage but not the bosses' staying power, and in the end short-term traders don't seem to do any better than long-term investors.

My second uncle loved to say: "Time is money. You have to control when money flows." In every crash he went around borrowing from people. What you borrow, he said, isn't money. It's time to tide yourself over.

Bosses can borrow time. Workers can't.

But borrowing time has a precondition: what you're waiting for has to actually come back. Hong Kong's "Shop King", Tang Shing-bor, spent most of his life borrowing time to hold on. Shop prices fell and rose again, and every time he made it through. After 2019, the protests, the emigration wave and the pandemic came one after another, and street-shop prices fell by nearly half from their peak. After his death, the family's properties went into receivership one after another, and both his estate administrators and his son were served bankruptcy petitions ([HKET](https://news.cnyes.com/news/id/6071482), [Sing Tao](https://www.stheadline.com/realtime-property/3560799)). When what you're waiting for doesn't come back, borrowed time is just debt.

My second uncle's son later ran his business by his father's rules too, and ended up bankrupt. What he didn't foresee was that the times had changed, and his clients had become people of a different background. In the new environment, some clients could owe you money and genuinely never pay; if you wanted to chase them, you didn't know what you could still rely on. It wasn't just the clients that changed. The rules, and the people enforcing them, changed too. He took on too much of this business. On paper he had a great deal of money, all of it bad debt.

Tang Shing-bor was waiting for his assets to recover. My cousin was waiting for money that was never going to come. No amount of borrowed time could bring it back.

Having a business doesn't make you safe, but it gives you a switch that workers don't have: if you can't hold on, you lay people off. Layoffs, pay cuts, late wages, downsizing — push the pressure down and protect your own cash flow first. A worker can't flip that switch, because he's the one who gets laid off.

TV dramas and novels love to show bosses losing everything in a crash and ending in misery. A small-business owner really can end up like that: the business is his entire fortune, and if it can't hold, he's finished. But once a business is a little bigger, it's a completely different league: cash flow from the business underneath, a bank relationship to negotiate with, staff to cut. When the storm passes, the people who actually bear the losses are usually the ones below.

That game was over by the early 2000s. More and more buyers stopped paying up front and moved to paying months after delivery, so businesses ended up fronting money to their customers. A business used to be leverage that made money for you; later the business itself needed your money to stay afloat.

## The Boss Drove a Ford, the Second Generation a Porsche

Ironically, the salaried workers who emigrated loved driving Mercedes, Porsches and Ferraris, while the bosses were tight-fisted to a fault. Not for lack of money; they just wanted everything practical and useful. When they bought a car, they bought a Ford.

The boss was frugal because he faced the real numbers every day and knew how many deals it took to earn a sum back. A car, to him, wasn't status. It was an expense that would never make money again. A worker had no business; his salary was everything, so a car became the most visible sign of success.

As a kid I heard a cousin say his father wouldn't let him buy a Porsche and told him to buy a Ford. In the end his mother stepped in and bought him the Porsche. One household already ran on two logics of money.

Many of these bosses' children grew up abroad. The family business had long been sold or wound up, and there was nothing to take over. Many ended up in government jobs or some other stable, easy position. That cousin with the Porsche finished a master's degree and went into government procurement — an easy job, with time to buy this and that.

The same big chamber-of-commerce family: the one who stayed in Hong Kong was my second uncle's son, who after his bankruptcy moved into public housing and worked in a convenience store; the one who emigrated with a large sum of money worked for the government abroad. One down on his luck, one secure, but both roads ended in the same place: nobody ran a business anymore, and they all became people working for someone else. The only difference was whether they got the money out in time.

Whether they later did well or badly doesn't really matter. What matters is that the business, and the skill of reading the numbers, stopped with the previous generation. The money the bosses saved bought their children a secure life, but the ledger kept in their heads was never passed on.

## I Forgot the Abacus, but Kept the Way of Reading Numbers

Nearly forty years later, I can no longer use an abacus. The multiplication, division and ratios my father taught me, I can't remember any of it.

But I remember which numbers went on the abacus and which on the calculator; that some numbers were never written down; that someone saw clearly under his own table and then left at the best possible moment. None of this was taught on purpose. I picked it up standing by and watching.

Tools go out of date; the abacus was replaced by the computer long ago. But knowing which layer of the numbers is for others and which you keep to yourself; how to split money into instalments and keep ratios in check; how people with and without a business differ when the storm comes — none of that goes out of date.

I'm writing it down so my children can see it one day. Not so they'll follow the same path, but so they'll know that beyond the numbers on the books, there is always another ledger.
