# Taiwan Stocks Fell 24% in a Month, Yet It Can Only Be Called a "Correction": Crashes Haven't Disappeared, They've Been Spread Out

@Mythogen Engine · Nov 23, 2026

*Where Did the Crashes Go?* — Part 1 of 3

Taiwan's stock market fell 24 percent in a month, and US stocks suffered two sharp one-day drops within two weeks. Yet the market's official word is still "correction"; whoever says "crash" gets told they don't know what they're talking about. Crashes have not disappeared: they are averaged out by the index, erased by rebounds, caught by the national team, and then spread across the accounts of individual retail investors. They just aren't allowed to go by that name.

*Data as of 20 November 2026.*

## "Guessing costs nothing"

On 10 November, I said one thing in a group chat with friends: "The key is the 16th and 17th." My reasoning was that too many things were colliding in those few days: Nvidia was reporting earnings on the 17th, Taiwanese companies were reporting the same week, hedge funds had to file their holdings before the 16th, the market was pricing a December rate hike, and a great deal of leverage was concentrated in that window. The last line of my message was: "Not necessarily a crash, but it'll be quite a show."

It was quite a show. On 16 November the Nasdaq closed more than 10 percent below its 6 October high, officially entering correction territory; Nvidia closed at US$156.14, its lowest since July 2025 and about 35 percent off its peak. But no crash came. One friend replied with a crude Cantonese phrase that roughly means: "Guessing costs nothing."

By his way of keeping score, I lost, and I accept that. Short-term thinking asks "did you hit it or not": if you name a date, you have to deliver on that date. I was looking at something else: whether the conditions were in place. The two ways of keeping score measure different things. A stock market can be pushed up by stories, but the creditors who lend you money only care about the numbers.

This article is not a defence of that date. It asks a question that conversation left behind: if, by definition, crashes have in fact kept happening, why do they always get defined as "corrections," while the people who say "crash" are treated as the ones who don't understand?

## Measuring it against the definitions

There is no official definition of a stock market crash. The market uses only a rough yardstick: a 10 percent fall from the peak is a correction, a 20 percent fall is a bear market. "Crash" is usually reserved for a steep fall in a short time, such as Black Monday on 19 October 1987, when the Dow fell 22.6 percent in a single day.

Measure the past few months with that yardstick:

- **Taiwan**: The index hit an intraday record of 49,968 on 6 October; on 6 November its intraday low was 37,837 — a fall of 24.28 percent in one month. That day it dropped as much as 3,737 points intraday, the largest intraday point drop in its history.
- **South Korea**: In July, Samsung, SK hynix and other tech stocks lost a large share of their value within a short time. Retail investors who had borrowed heavily to buy faced huge losses, and several brokerages still could not recover receivables after forced liquidations.
- **United States**: On 5 November the S&P 500 fell 4.4 percent, its worst day since the "Liberation Day" tariffs of April 2025; the Nasdaq fell 5.5 percent.

A 24 percent fall in a month is bear-market territory by any yardstick; a one-day fall of more than 4 percent counts as a sharp drop in any era. By definition, both qualify. Yet the words used most in the news those days were "volatility," "pullback" and "retracement." Anyone who said "crash" at that point was most likely met with a correction of their own: "This is just a correction."

## Three corrections in six months, and the third broke the previous low

Stretch the timeframe to six months and Taiwan's shape becomes clearer:

1. **Late March**: a first fall of about 5,000 points.
2. **23 June to 30 July**: a fall of 8,800 points, or 18 percent.
3. **6 October to 6 November**: from 49,968 down to 37,837, breaking below the previous low of 39,384 set on 29 July, and losing the 240-day moving average at 38,784.
4. **Rebound, then down again**: a rebound to 44,257 on 11 November; then on 19 November a 4.4 percent fall to close at 38,427, with 993 listed stocks down and 128 of them limit-down.

After the first two falls, the index still went on to new highs. The third is different: for the first time, the low broke below the previous low. The second leg after the rebound was smaller than the first, yet it closed lower than the first, about 23 percent below the peak.

To be fair, as of 11 November Taiwan's market was still up about 52.8 percent for the year; on a full-year basis it was still a big bull market. What has changed is not the year's result but the shape of the past two months: three falls of close to 20 percent within one year, each called a "correction," and by the third, the floor began to give way.

The US is a step behind, but moving the same way. On 19 November the Nasdaq closed at 23,879, already below its 5 November level of 24,315. On 20 November the Nasdaq 100 fell 5.6 percent in a single day, its worst week since March 2020; the S&P 500 fell 3.7 percent, its seventh straight losing day, entering a technical correction. Nvidia fell 14 percent that day. Its chief executive, Jensen Huang, had already resigned on 12 November, and according to The Wall Street Journal the US Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating whether its disclosures misled investors.

## The index averages out the disaster

An index is an average, and what averages do best is hide the extremes.

On 5 November the semiconductor ETF (SMH) fell 11.2 percent in a day, and Nvidia, AMD, Micron and Qualcomm fell between 9 and 17 percent. At the close that same day, the S&P 500 was only 5.9 percent below its 6 October record. Look at the index and it was a pullback; look at the handful of stocks in your portfolio and it was a disaster.

The more the market is concentrated in a few AI stocks, the bigger this gap becomes. The index not falling 20 percent does not mean people heavily invested in tech did not fall 20 percent; the index not collapsing does not mean people who bought on margin were not liquidated. "Crash" is a word defined by the index, but disasters happen one person at a time.

## Why it can only be called a "correction"

It qualifies by definition, yet the official label is "correction." There are at least four reasons.

**First, the rebounds are too fast.** After Taiwan's sharp fall on 6 November, the market rebounded nearly 2,000 points on the next trading day (9 November); on 11 November it rose another 1,545 points, the sixth-largest closing gain in its history. Once a V-shaped rebound appears, the plunge gets rewritten as a brief "spike down," before the word "crash" has time to stick.

**Second, the decline is split into waves.** The word "crash" demands speed: the fall has to happen in a day or a few days. Fall slowly in several waves and the total decline can be the same or even larger, yet no single wave is "disastrous" enough; each is only called "volatility" or a "slow correction."

**Third, someone is buying.** On 6 November, Taiwan's National Stabilization Fund announced its tenth intervention to support the market, with up to NT$500 billion available, and one of the reasons it gave was "sustained net foreign selling." In Hong Kong, Southbound money from the mainland bought against the trend: on 11 November the Hang Seng fell 0.98 percent, yet Southbound funds were still net buyers of about HK$4.573 billion, accounting for 43.29 percent of Hang Seng turnover.

**Fourth, leverage did not trigger cascading margin calls.** Historical crashes often involved forced liquidations triggering forced selling, which triggered more liquidations. This time, margin balances fell by NT$56.4 billion in a single day, mostly margin traders leaving on their own; on the day of the rebound, only 81 accounts were force-liquidated.

Put the four together and the crash is broken into something else: a series of falls not dramatic enough, each caught by someone, each followed by a rebound you can point to and say: "See, it's just a correction."

## Labels follow the narrative

If "crash" is not decided by definition, what decides it? During this sell-off, I ran a small test without meaning to.

I gave an AI assistant half a sentence of optimism, and it called the market "healthy digestion within a bull market." Three days later, on 22 November, I pasted the same AI the transcript of a Hong Kong finance YouTuber's video about the sell-off. The video's main line was "this is definitely not a technical correction." The AI's very first verdict was "a watershed in nature"; when I pressed further, it said the market had "officially entered a crash," and listed what it called "three red lines." Those three red lines are not a definition the market commonly uses; the only common one is "a 10 percent fall is a correction, a 20 percent fall is a bear market."

The same product gave the same person two opposite verdicts within three days, both stated with confidence. What changed in between was not only the market but what I pasted into it.

And on that same Friday, mainstream financial reporting was still saying "technical correction." Put the three together: the news says correction, the YouTuber says it isn't a correction, and the AI follows its input from "healthy digestion" to "crash." The numbers are the same numbers; the label is decided by whoever is telling the story.

A label is also an identity. In group chats and on the financial pages, "correction" is an insider's word and "crash" is an outsider's. Say correction and you sound calm and in command of the definitions; say crash and you get called clueless, or treated as someone who has panicked. So even after Taiwan's market had fallen more than 20 percent and qualified as a bear market, people still chose the milder word. A definition that was meant for measuring has become, here, a tool for correcting other people.

This brings us back to the two ways of keeping score from the start: stories decide the short-term name; arithmetic decides the long-term result. A market was called both "healthy digestion" and a "crash," yet its lows just kept getting lower, wave after wave.

## Who bears the losses

No one calling it a crash does not mean no one is losing money. It is just that the losses are not concentrated on one day or in one institution; they are spread across many people's accounts.

A classic Taiwanese study shows where that money goes. Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean used the complete trading records of every investor on the Taiwan Stock Exchange and found that individual investors as a group lost 3.8 percentage points of return a year through trading, losses equal to 2.2 percent of Taiwan's GDP. Over the same period, institutional investors beat the market by 1.5 percentage points. The researchers put it bluntly: foreign investors' profits were a transfer of wealth from Taiwanese retail investors to foreign institutions.

This sell-off had a live version too. At limit-down, retail investors were force-liquidated, and foreign investors bought 5,036 lots of Formosa SUMCO Technology in a single day to take the shares. At the same price, one side was forced to sell and the other chose to buy.

Retail investors often do not feel they are losing. Each trade loses only a little: a bit of commission, buying a bit too high, getting out a bit too late. They take profits when they have them and hold on to losses without admitting them, which behavioural finance calls the "disposition effect." They remember the winning trades clearly; the losing ones "haven't been sold yet, so they don't count." Once a crash is broken into small pieces, so are the losses — small enough that everyone can shrug them off.

## Conclusion: whose shoulders did it land on?

Back to that line in the group chat: "Guessing costs nothing." By short-term scoring, I guessed wrong that time, and there is no need to argue the point. But by structural scoring, what happened in those days and after illustrates something bigger: crashes have not disappeared; they have changed from "a one-day collapse" into "losses that are spread out." They are averaged out by the index, erased by rebounds, caught by the national team and Southbound money, and then broken into a series of declines that may only be called "corrections."

Losses that are spread out are still losses. The next question is: whose shoulders did they land on? Foreign investors can leave — who can't? That is what the next article sets out to answer.

This series does not predict dates; it only discusses conditions: who gets hurt, how badly, and how to prepare. Why that is still useful without dates is left for the third article.
