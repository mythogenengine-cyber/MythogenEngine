# Why You Can't See the Crash: The Risk Has Moved into Your MPF

@Mythogen Engine · Nov 23, 2026

*Where Did the Crashes Go?* — Part 2 of 3

The losses called "corrections" have not disappeared; they have been moved. They have moved from foreign investors, who can leave, to local retail investors, pension funds and national teams, who cannot. Leverage, meanwhile, has moved from the balance sheet into the footnotes.

*Data as of 20 November 2026.*

## Who the losses were spread to

The previous article concluded that crashes have not disappeared; they have been averaged out by the index, erased by rebounds, and then broken into a series of declines that may only be called "corrections."

Losses that are spread out are still losses. The most direct way to find out whose shoulders they landed on is to look at who was selling and who was buying while the market fell. Every trade has two sides: when someone hands risk off, someone else takes it on.

This article argues that over the past few months, in both Taiwan and Hong Kong, those handing off the risk were mostly people who could leave at any time; those taking it on were mostly people with no choice, some of whom did not even know they were taking it.

## Foreign money exits, local money takes the shares

Start with Taiwan. In October, foreign investors were net sellers of NT$994.3 billion of Taiwan stocks, the largest monthly figure on record; in the first six days of November they sold another NT$524.4 billion net. By 17 November, foreign investors' cumulative net selling for the year was approaching NT$3.1 trillion.

The strange part is that while foreign investors sold three trillion, Taiwan's market was at one point up more than 50 percent for the year. The main buyers were domestic fund houses, with cumulative net purchases of about NT$1.19 trillion over the same period. Where does the fund houses' money come from? Largely from retail investors buying ETFs. In other words, the shares foreign investors handed off ended up, through the ETF channel, in the hands of Taiwanese retail investors.

Foreign investors were not simply leaving, either. While cutting their cash-market holdings heavily, they still held a net short position of nearly 98,000 contracts in Taiwan index futures. On one side, they sold the shares to locals; on the other, they bet the shares would fall.

Hong Kong's mechanism is different, but the result is similar. There, the buyer is Southbound money from the mainland. On 11 November the Hang Seng fell 0.98 percent, yet Southbound funds were still net buyers of about HK$4.573 billion against the trend, accounting for 43.29 percent of Hang Seng turnover. More than 40 percent of Hong Kong's trading now comes from mainland money. But Southbound money does not only prop the market up: when Hong Kong stocks rebounded on 5 November, Southbound funds were instead net sellers of about HK$21.3 billion, a seven-month one-day high. Buy on the falls, sell on the rebounds: propping up the market is also a business.

This is the shape I described in [*Exit Mechanism Trilogy: The Cost of Cheapness*](../exit-mechanism/退出機制三部曲.md): the right to exit is not evenly distributed. Foreign investors can leave, and when they leave someone takes their shares; the locals who take them often cannot leave.

## Compulsory entry, no exit

Retail investors buying ETFs at least chose to do so. There is a group of people who get no choice at all: pension contributors.

Take Hong Kong's Mandatory Provident Fund (MPF). The rules are clear:

- **No choice about joining**: employers and employees each contribute 5 percent of relevant income, capped at HK$1,500 each for monthly income above HK$30,000.
- **The money is locked until 65**: withdrawals are generally only allowed at age 65, with a few exceptions such as early retirement, permanently leaving Hong Kong, total incapacity or terminal illness.
- **Limited transfers**: under the Employee Choice Arrangement, you can transfer only once per calendar year, the whole balance in one go, and only the employee's portion; the employer's contributions stay in the original scheme.
- **Choose from the menu only**: you can only pick funds offered by your trustee; you cannot hold cash or buy shares yourself.

Total MPF assets were about HK$1.55 trillion at the end of 2025. Money goes in automatically every month, contributions continue through falling markets, and the money cannot be taken out — this passivity makes it the most stable buyer in the market. The Default Investment Strategy, popularly known as the "lazy fund," took in about 40 percent of net inflows this year, and funds of this kind rebalance automatically: when stocks fall, they sell bonds to buy stocks.

2008 demonstrated the price of this passivity. According to MPFA annual reports, the MPF's annualised return since December 2000 was still 7.4 percent as of March 2008; a year later it had fallen to minus 0.8 percent. Total assets fell from HK$248.2 billion to HK$217.74 billion, even with HK$43.4 billion of new contributions that year. After eight years of contributions, members were in the red even counting returns.

Taiwan is more direct: pension money is itself the national team's ammunition. The National Stabilization Fund can draw on the four major government funds, and the Fund itself says that these four funds mainly manage workers' and civil servants' retirement and savings money, with a borrowing cap of NT$300 billion. Most of the Fund's past interventions have been profitable; one analyst described those profits as having been earned from the "collapsed" confidence of retail investors.

To be fair: people who kept contributing after 2008 and did not leave midway mostly made it back. Those really hurt were mainly two groups: people who happened to retire in a crash year and needed to take their money out; and people who panicked at the lows and switched to conservative funds. So the problem is not "everyone loses together" but three things holding at once: the risk is shared by the whole population, the fees are charged regardless, and the timing risk falls on individuals.

## Not just Hong Kong

Pushing workers' retirement savings into the stock market is standard practice in almost every mature market:

- **Taiwan**: under the new labor pension system, employers must contribute at least 6 percent of wages each month, and workers may voluntarily contribute up to another 6 percent.
- **United States**: 401(k)s and IRAs; many companies enrol employees automatically by default, and most of the money flows into target-date funds and index funds.
- **Australia**: the compulsory employer superannuation guarantee rose to 12 percent of wages from 1 July 2025.
- **Japan**: the GPIF, one of the world's largest pension funds, raised its target for Japanese equities from 12 percent to 25 percent in 2014; today domestic and foreign stocks and bonds each make up a quarter.
- **South Korea and Malaysia**: the National Pension Service (NPS) and the Employees Provident Fund (EPF) are both major investors in their local stock markets, and are often seen as national teams when markets fall.

What this money has in common: it flows in automatically every month, mostly buys according to the index, and does not look at price. In normal times it is long-term capital; in falling markets it is the buyer of last resort; in Taiwan it is even direct ammunition for the national team.

Moreover, buying according to the index means buying according to the index's concentration. In the GPIF's disclosed holdings for fiscal 2025, the top three foreign stocks were Nvidia, Apple and Microsoft. A Japanese worker's retirement savings and Silicon Valley's AI story are tied together just like that. Someone else decides what gets bought, but the contributors bear the risk.

## Leverage moved into the footnotes

Risk has not only moved from one group of people to another; it has also moved from places you can see to places you cannot.

Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Oracle together have US$1.65 trillion of "off-balance-sheet" debt, more than the US$1.35 trillion on their balance sheets. What is written in the main body of the financial statements is no longer the whole story.

On the front line of the AI supply chain, borrowing costs have started to speak. CoreWeave's unsecured bonds issued between May 2025 and June 2026 carry coupons of 9 to 9.75 percent, but now trade at market yields of around 15 percent, with some above 16 percent. A stock market can be held up by stories; the people who lend you money only care about the numbers.

The big companies are no longer "sit back and collect cash" businesses either. Alphabet's third-quarter free cash flow was minus US$9.1 billion, its second straight negative quarter and the first time in more than twenty years as a listed company. Anthropic, listed for less than a week, states in its prospectus that it has committed to about US$895 billion of future spending, almost all on computing and data centres — seven times its US$132 billion in cash and short-term investments; many of the contracts must be paid whether or not the capacity is used. The spending is hard; the revenue is soft.

This is what I said in [*Where Does the Chaos Go?*](../chaos-series/00-preface.md): chaos does not disappear, it only moves. The problem in Hong Kong's 1973 crash was fake shares sitting right in front of you; today's problem is leverage hidden in footnotes you will never read. And the pension funds holding the index have a share in all of it.

## Mutual guarantees: a loop

Once leverage has moved into the footnotes, there is a layer that is even harder to see: companies guaranteeing one another.

Nvidia's approach is to take stakes in its customers, or guarantee their debts, and the customers then use that money to buy Nvidia hardware. It holds about 7 percent of CoreWeave and signed an agreement with an initial value of US$6.3 billion committing to buy whatever computing capacity CoreWeave cannot rent out: if CoreWeave cannot find tenants, Nvidia is the buyer of last resort. This year it invested US$30 billion in OpenAI, and has discussed guaranteeing up to US$250 billion of OpenAI's data-centre leases, plus financing US$350 billion of chip purchases.

AMD's version uses its own shares instead of cash. It gave OpenAI warrants for up to 160 million shares, about 10 percent of the company, at one cent per share, with the final tranche vesting only if AMD's share price reaches US$600; it later gave Meta similar terms. It has also committed to invest up to US$5 billion in Anthropic, on condition that Anthropic deploys AMD chips. Google, for its part, has guaranteed the leases on five of Anthropic's data centres, enabling the latter to obtain the equivalent of US$35 billion in loans.

One commentator put it this way: in the world of AI, everyone owes everyone; once ChatGPT's growth stalls, mutual support turns into mutual drag. The International Monetary Fund and the Bank for International Settlements have both listed AI circular financing as a systemic downside risk.

The problem lies with the guarantors themselves. Nvidia's guarantees are contingent liabilities that will be triggered when AI slows down — which is exactly when Nvidia's own orders fall. And Nvidia has run into trouble of its own. On 12 November, chief executive Jensen Huang resigned while the board was running an independent investigation into reports that he had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort; according to The Wall Street Journal, the US Securities and Exchange Commission has also opened a civil probe, examining in part whether the company's disclosures misled investors. Its share price is down about 35 percent from its peak. The biggest guarantor of all changed its commander in the very two weeks it was most needed.

So if Nvidia cannot hold, will AMD step in? Hardly. AMD's "guarantees" are paid out through its share price; they only mean something if the price goes up. On 5 November, AMD fell on the same day as Nvidia, Micron and Qualcomm, by between 9 and 17 percent; on 19 November, after a short-seller went public with a bearish call, AMD fell another 9.2 percent. The moment a guarantee is most needed is exactly the moment it is least able to deliver.

The guarantors and the guaranteed are betting on the same story: in good times they add leverage to each other; in bad times they fail together. This is another reason you cannot see the crash: the risk has not been eliminated but locked between the parties by guarantee contracts, and it will only surface all at once when one of them can no longer hold.

## Hong Kong: a fundraising channel

Hong Kong has one more layer of structure, which only makes sense once you look at its place in the national plan.

The outline of China's 15th Five-Year Plan gives "promoting the long-term prosperity and stability of Hong Kong and Macao" a chapter of its own, and expands Hong Kong's role from the "eight centres" of the 14th plan to "ten centres," "two hubs" and "one ecosystem." Hong Kong's own first five-year plan (2026–2030) positions the city as a channel and bridge connecting the country with the world.

The data show what this "channel" actually does. In the first half of this year, mainland companies accounted for 96.4 percent of new listings in Hong Kong and 98.5 percent of the funds raised. Hong Kong's IPO fundraising is back near the top of the global rankings, but as of 15 September, 82 of this year's 108 new listings had already fallen below their offer price.

The fundraising side got the money; the side buying the new shares took the stock. If the main function of Hong Kong's stock market is to help companies raise funds, then whether the Hang Seng rises and whether Hong Kong investors make money are not the most important measures; whether the fundraising function works is.

Officials will describe this role as a "bridge" and an "international financial centre"; critics will say Hong Kong stocks have become a cash machine. Both describe the same set of data. For workers holding MPF accounts invested in Hong Kong equity funds, the difference hardly matters: the glamour on paper belongs to the fundraisers, and the risk stays with those who take the shares.

## Conclusion: how long can the holders hold?

Put the threads of this article together: foreign investors are selling, and local retail investors are buying through ETFs; Southbound money buys on the falls and sells on the rebounds; pension funds buy automatically every month, and in Taiwan are directly the national team's ammunition; leverage has moved into the footnotes of financial statements; and Hong Kong's stock market runs mainly to raise funds.

The risk has not disappeared; it has moved onto people who have no choice. That is why you cannot see the crash: not because it has not happened, but because every time it happens, someone holds it up. And those holding it up very likely include your MPF or labor pension account.

The next question is: how long can the holders hold? The national team's ammunition has a ceiling, central banks cannot print freely amid high inflation, and every rescue leaves more risk in the rescuers' hands. That is what the final article sets out to answer.
